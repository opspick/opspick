Best booking software for nutritionists

The first appointment is the product

Booking tools treat every appointment as interchangeable. Nutrition does not work that way. There is an initial consultation that needs a long slot and a completed intake form, and there are follow-ups that need twenty minutes and a look at last month's notes.

  • Initial and follow-up as distinct services — different length, price and preparation.
  • An intake form tied to the first booking — sent on payment, not after.
  • Consent captured once — and stored where you can find it.
  • Video or in-person — the same client may switch between them.
  • Programme packages — three months, six sessions, one invoice.

The three tools, honestly

Fresha

Free and pleasant for clients, and the form builder is enough to capture basic intake. Long appointments are fine. What it will not do is hold a structured food diary, a measurement history or a set of clinical notes — so plan on a second tool for the actual work.

Booksy

Better at reminders and no-show recovery, which matters when a no-show is ninety minutes of your week. Packages exist but the flow is retail-shaped: buy a block, redeem sessions. Recurring follow-ups every four weeks can be set up and are tedious to maintain across a full client list.

Square Appointments

Strongest on money and no-show fees, and the calendar is genuinely clear. Forms are basic. If you sell programmes at a fixed price with a deposit, this is the least fiddly of the three for billing.

Forms, consent and the paperwork before the call

An intake form completed in advance changes the consultation. You arrive knowing about the medication, the allergies, the shift work, the previous attempt that failed. Completed at the start of the call, it eats fifteen minutes of a ninety-minute slot.

So the ordering rule is simple: pay, then form, then appointment. If the tool cannot enforce that order, send the form manually on confirmation — it is one message and it is worth sending.

Consent is the part people forget. If you are giving advice about food and health, you want a record that the client understood the scope and the limits. A checkbox on a booking form is thin; a separate signed document is better. Decide which one you would be comfortable defending, and store it accordingly.

Programmes, packages and the third session

Most nutrition income comes from programmes, not single sessions. That means the booking tool has to express "six sessions over three months, paid up front, with the option to reschedule twice". Many tools can do the payment. Fewer handle the reschedule allowance without a manual note.

The third session is where programmes quietly die. The first is motivating, the second is a check-in, the third is a Tuesday in week seven when nothing feels different. Software cannot fix adherence, but a reminder that sounds like a person rather than a system helps. Write the message yourself once and reuse it.

Also decide what happens when a client stops booking. A programme with four unused sessions is either revenue you keep, an obligation you owe, or a refund conversation. Put the answer in the terms before you sell the first one, and make sure the booking system agrees with the terms.

What I would do

Solo practitioner, in-person and video, selling three-month programmes: Square Appointments for the deposit and the invoice, with intake handled by a form tool you control. The billing clarity is worth more than the extra features elsewhere.

Just starting out and price-sensitive: Fresha on the free tier, two services, form attached to the initial consult. Move when you have more than about fifteen active clients and the follow-up scheduling starts to slip.

If most of your work is remote and notes-heavy, Booksy for reminders plus a dedicated practice-management tool is a reasonable pair — but check current pricing, because two subscriptions add up faster than one.
