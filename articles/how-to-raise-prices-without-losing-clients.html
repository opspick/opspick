How to raise prices without losing clients

A price rise is not a negotiation with your whole client list. It is one clear message, sent early, to people who already decided they like you.

Start with the arithmetic, not the announcement

Most solo operators under-price by 15–25% and assume a rise will empty the calendar. Run the numbers before you write anything. If you raise a £60 service to £66 and lose one client in ten, you keep more money and work fewer hours. At £60, losing two clients costs £120 a week — a 10% rise only pays that back if you keep more than nine in ten.

So the rule is simple. A 10% rise can survive roughly one loss in ten. A 20% rise can survive two. Anything above that is a repositioning, and you should price it like one.

Four signs it is actually time

  • You are booked 3–4 weeks out at current prices. Demand above your capacity is the strongest single signal.
  • Your costs moved — product, rent, card fees, fuel. This is the easiest rise to explain because it is true and verifiable.
  • You have not raised prices in 18 months. Inflation alone has eaten 5–8% of your margin.

What is not a signal: one quiet week, a competitor's price list, or a bad month. Raise on structure, not on mood.

The notice period

Four to six weeks is the sweet spot for recurring clients. Long enough that nobody feels ambushed, short enough that you do not spend two months apologising. One clean message, at a fixed date, before the price changes — not a slow drip of hints.

If you take deposits or sell prepaid packages, decide the date line now: rises apply to bookings made after the date, and to packages bought after it. Anything already paid stays at the old price. Ambiguity here is what turns a rise into a refund argument.

Who gets grandfathered, and for how long

Grandfathering is a discount you are choosing to give, so give it a deadline. Three options, in order of how much goodwill they buy:

  1. Nobody. Fastest, cleanest, and defensible if your prices were genuinely below market. Expect to lose 5–10%.
  2. Everyone, for three months. The default. Buys you time and softens the message without becoming permanent.
  3. Long-standing clients only, for six to twelve months. Best retention, worst admin — you need a note on each client record, or you will honour the wrong price in month four.

Whichever you pick, write it down in one sentence and use the same sentence with everyone. Inconsistent grandfathering is the thing clients actually resent, more than the number.

The message that works

Four parts, under 120 words: what is changing, from when, why (one clause), and what stays the same. No apology, no essay, no "unfortunately".

Hi [name] — from 1 March my prices go up by about 10%: a cut is £42 instead of £38. Product and rent costs have moved a lot this year and this keeps the appointment length and the time I spend with you exactly as it is. Anything you book before 1 March stays at today's price. Thanks for being one of the regulars — it genuinely matters.

Send it as a personal message to regulars and as a short note on the booking page for everyone else. Do not bury it in a newsletter with three other items; people miss it and then feel tricked at the till.

The day it goes live

Update every surface on the same day: booking page, price list at the desk, Google Business Profile services, Instagram highlights, and the quote template. If you sell memberships or packages, check that the per-visit maths still beats the single price, or the plan quietly stops selling.

Handling pushback

Most pushback is a question in disguise. Answer the question, do not defend the decision. "That's a big jump" usually means "is anything changing for me?" — so say what stays the same. For the client who says they will leave, let them go without a discount. A discount resets the anchor for everyone who hears about it, and they always hear.

Keep one concession in reserve and use it once: a shorter appointment at a lower price, or a small add-on bundled in for the first cycle. Not a percentage off.

The mistakes that cause the churn

  • Raising prices quietly and letting the till announce it.
  • Two rises in six months. One bigger, well-explained rise is kinder than two small surprises.
  • Apologising so much the client starts to think they are doing you a favour.

Then track bookings for four weeks. Revenue per hour up and the calendar still 80% full means you priced correctly. Half empty means you went too far in one step — hold the price and rebuild volume with rebooking rather than rolling it back.

Frequently asked

How much notice should I give for a price increase?

Four to six weeks for regular clients, in one clear message with the date and the new price. Longer than that invites negotiation; shorter feels like a surprise.

Should I grandfather existing clients?

Usually for three months, or six to twelve for long-standing regulars. Put an end date on it, note it on the client record, and apply the same rule to everyone.

What percentage price increase is safe?

10% is broadly safe and can absorb losing about one client in ten. 20% is a repositioning: expect more churn.
