Best booking software for dog walkers

Most dog walking businesses are not struggling with bookings. They are struggling with routes, keys and the monthly invoice for forty repeats.

What a dog walker actually books

It looks like a calendar and behaves like a delivery route. The pattern is almost always the same:

  • Repeat walks at fixed times. "Monday to Friday, 12:00, thirty minutes" — one booking that covers months, with holidays and sick days taken out.
  • Group walks with a cap. Four dogs per walker is a business decision; the tool has to enforce it or you will break it by accident on a busy Friday.
  • Meet-and-greet before the first walk. A short, free, in-person visit. Without it, the first paid walk is also the trial.
  • Keys and codes. Somebody has to record who holds a key, which door, which alarm code — and when it changed.

Where the usual three land

First, the part no tool solves: routes. Grouping walks by neighbourhood is the difference between seven hours of work and nine hours of driving. Schedule by time block, use the client address field as your routing input, and sequence by area yourself once a week.

Mostly recurring walks for a dozen regulars? A general tool with solid recurring bookings and card on file is enough. Ten walkers and two hundred dogs? You need a pet-care platform priced per walker, worth it only at that scale.

Keys, access and trust

This is where dog walkers get hurt, and no booking software fixes it. Keep a separate access record — a simple table is fine — with the dog, the address, the key type, the alarm code and who is currently holding it. Update it the day anything changes, not at the end of the month.

Two rules that prevent most problems:

  1. Never store alarm or door codes in the booking notes. Notes get exported, emailed and screenshotted. Keep codes in one place with access limited to the people who need them.
  2. Confirm the first walk in writing. Time window, duration, what happens in rain, and whether the dog goes out with others. A one-paragraph confirmation avoids most first-week disputes.

Weather, cancellations and billing the regulars

You need a published rule, not a negotiation every storm. Decide what happens in heavy rain, extreme heat and snow, write it into the service agreement, and make the app enforce it: same-day cancellations by the client are charged, weather cancellations by you are not. The cancellation window guide has wording for both sides.

Then bill the regulars. Card on file, charged weekly or on the first of the month, is the biggest admin saving in this trade. Invoice-per-walk sounds fair and turns into twenty messages and three late payers. Sell packages if you like, but track them in the tool, not in your head — untracked packages cause most "I paid for eleven, you did nine" arguments.

Write down your week: how many repeats, how many one-offs, how many dogs per walk, how you bill. Then test the tool against exactly that — create one recurring five-day walk, add a second dog to the same slot, take a payment on file and cancel one walk. If any of those four steps needs a workaround you cannot live with daily, pick the next tool. The feature list is irrelevant next to that test.

Frequently asked

Do I need pet-specific software to start walking dogs?

No. With a handful of regular clients, a general booking tool with recurring appointments and card on file covers it. Pet-specific platforms make sense once you employ walkers.

How should I charge regular clients?

Weekly or monthly billing with a card on file. Per-walk invoicing creates chasing work and late payments for no benefit.
