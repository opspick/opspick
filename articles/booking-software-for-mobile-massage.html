Booking software for mobile massage therapists

In a clinic, the room is ready before the client arrives. On a mobile call, you are the room, the linen service and the receptionist, and the calendar has to account for all three.

What changes when the table moves

A 60-minute massage is not a 60-minute booking once you leave the building. It is a treatment plus setup, pack-down, and a journey that may or may not have parking at the end. Software that models only the treatment will produce a day that looks fine on screen and is impossible in practice.

  • Setup is part of the appointment. Ten to fifteen minutes before, the same after: table, linens, oils, towels, a wipe-down of the floor space.
  • The address is a clinical detail. Stairs, a lift, a narrow hallway, a room that fits a table only one way. You need it at 8am, not in a message thread.
  • Intake cannot happen at the door. Health screening needs to be read before you set up, not while the client is standing in a dressing gown.
  • The client may need time afterwards. Some people get up slowly. Booking back-to-back with a five-minute gap is how a schedule starts running late at 11am and never recovers.
  • Signal is not guaranteed. Basements, lifts, rural roads. The day view has to survive being offline.

Tools that fit, and where each one strains

General booking platforms can all be made to work. The question is how much manual correction you accept every morning, and whether the tool understands that your premises change address four times a day.

  • Fresha. A free calendar with a strong client app and a service menu that handles add-ons and packages reasonably well. It assumes a fixed business address by default, so the location has to become a per-appointment field you fill without fail. Check current plans.
  • Booksy. Reliable reminders, deposits at booking, and a mobile app that is comfortable one-handed. Reminders matter more for mobile work than for a shop, because a no-show wastes a journey as well as a slot. Check current pricing.
  • Square Appointments. The cheapest credible start, with reminders included and card payments in the same app where you would take a deposit. Group and multi-person bookings are the weak spot. Check current plans.
  • A calendar plus a form. Google Calendar and a form tool will run a small mobile practice honestly, provided you block travel and setup yourself and keep deposits manual. It fails at the point where you cannot remember who has paid for a five-session block.

Pricing the journey without hiding it

Decide one rule and publish it: travel included inside a radius, a flat zone fee, or a slightly higher price that absorbs the driving. All three are defensible. A surprise charge at the door is not, and it is the complaint that follows a therapist into every new client.

Show the fee as a line item where the software allows it, and make the radius concrete in the booking flow — "within 10 miles of the studio" beats "local area". If you serve offices with chair massage, price per therapist-hour rather than per person, because a 15-minute slot still costs you the setup and the journey.

Intake, consent and notes that survive a driveway

Send the health form with the confirmation, and set it to be completed before the appointment rather than on arrival. Ask the questions that change what you do: pregnancy, recent surgery, blood pressure, anticoagulants, injuries, areas to avoid. Keep the timestamp.

Consent for draping and for any area the client wants skipped should be recorded, not remembered. After the session, write notes where you will find them next month — on the client record, not on paper in the car.
