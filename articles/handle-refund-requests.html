How to handle refund requests without losing the client

Almost nobody asks for a refund because they enjoy the conversation. They ask because something did not match what they expected, and how you respond in the first two minutes decides whether you keep them.

Separate the two kinds of request

There is a difference between a refund for a service the client received and disliked, and a refund for something that did not happen — a cancelled appointment, a double charge, a deposit taken in error. The first is a judgement call about quality. The second is arithmetic, and it should be settled fast and without argument.

  • Duplicate or erroneous charge: refund immediately, no conditions. Anything else damages trust for no gain.
  • Service not delivered as booked: refund or redo, your choice offered to them.
  • Service delivered, client unhappy with the result: assess, then decide between fix, credit and refund.
  • Change of mind after the service: normally no refund, but say so kindly.

Getting the category right before you reply keeps you from negotiating a situation that has no negotiation in it. Refunding a duplicate charge is not a concession; it is correcting your own error.

Three questions to ask first

You need facts without sounding like an interrogation. Three questions cover most cases: what did you expect, what happened instead, and when did you first notice. The third tells you whether this is a genuine disappointment or an afterthought.

Then check your own record: what was booked, who performed it, what notes exist, whether the client was told the relevant limitations at the time. If your notes are thin, say so and lean towards the client. Arguing from an empty file is how small disputes become public ones.

Offer in the right order

The order of your offers matters more than their size. Most clients would rather have the problem fixed than have their money back, and offering a fix first signals that you believe the work can be made right.

Two things to avoid: offering a credit when the client has clearly asked for money, and offering money when a quick redo would have solved it. Both read as negotiation tactics rather than answers.

Say it in plain words

A short written reply is worth more than a long phone call, because the client can reread it and you both have a record. Something like: "I've looked at your booking — you're right that the appointment was shorter than booked. I can refund the difference today, or redo the service at no charge next week, whichever suits you better."

Note what it does not do: explain your costs, speculate about motives, or apologise for a policy. It states the finding, offers two options, and stops. Long justifications invite replies, and replies invite escalation.

If you are declining, decline once and clearly: what the policy is, why it applies here, and what you can offer instead. Repeating the same refusal in three messages is what turns a disappointed client into an angry one.

Decide the money question in advance

Refunds decided under pressure are inconsistent, and inconsistency is what clients compare notes about. Set a threshold below which any staff member can refund without asking — many businesses use the price of a standard appointment — and require a second person above it.

Also decide how refunds are paid. Card refunds take days and cannot be reversed to cash; deposits taken through a booking platform usually have to go back through the same route. If your terms do not say this, clients assume cash on the spot. Square and Booksy both return deposits through the original payment route; check current pricing. Keep the record, and the lesson

Log every refund and why, in one place. After a quarter, read the list. If the same service, staff member or time of day keeps appearing, the refunds are a symptom of something you can fix — an intake question not asked, a price expectation not set, a treatment sold to the wrong client.

That review is also where you find the wording to add to your booking confirmation. Most refund requests are preventable at the point of booking, with one honest sentence about what the service does and does not include. Fixing that reduces volume more than any refund policy.
