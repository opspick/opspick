How to collect deposits without card fees

A deposit is meant to protect the booking, not to hand 4 percent of it to a processor. There are cheaper rails, and each one fails in a different place.

The fee is a percentage of the deposit, not of the job

This is the number most owners never run. A $120 appointment with a 25 percent deposit means the client pays $30 now, and a card fee of 2.9 percent plus 30 cents takes $1.17. That is 3.9 percent of the money you actually collected, not the 2.9 percent quoted. The fixed part is the problem, and it hurts on every small deposit.

Run it across a year. Four hundred deposits at card rates costs about $470. The same four hundred on a bank rail at 0.8 percent costs about $96. Four hundred dollars that stays in the business and buys nothing different.

The four rails, cheapest first

  1. Bank transfer or direct debit. In the US, Stripe ACH Direct Debit charges 0.8 percent capped at $5. In the UK and eurozone, GoCardless is around 1 percent plus a small fixed fee, capped per transaction. Cheapest at scale, slowest to clear.
  2. Instant bank apps. Zelle, Interac e-Transfer and ordinary bank transfers cost nothing or close to it, and land in minutes. No processor, no chargeback, no reconciliation feed.
  3. Payment apps with a business account. Venmo business charges 1.9 percent plus 10 cents, Cash App business 2.75 percent. Cheaper than cards, instant, awkward on invoices.
  4. Cards. Square, Stripe, PayPal and your booking tool's checkout sit in the 2.6 to 3.5 percent range plus a fixed fee. The only rail that reliably handles disputes, refunds and saved cards in one place.

What each rail does to your cash flow

Cost is only half the decision. A card deposit settles within two business days, before the appointment. ACH takes three to five days and can bounce a week later, so the booking you thought you secured was never secured. Bank apps are instant but manual: someone has to read the notification and tick the client off. Ten minutes a day of matching transfers to names is forty hours a year, and the 2 percent you saved is gone.

Where the cheap rails actually break

  • No chargeback, so no leverage. A bank transfer cannot be reversed. That protects you from a false dispute, and equally protects a client from a refund you promised and did not send. Your written policy has to be good, because the payment rail will not referee.
  • No saved card for the balance. Bank rails collect the deposit and nothing else. The remaining $90 is still collected at the counter, and a client who does not turn up still owes it.
  • Names do not match. A transfer from an account in someone else's name is your daily reconciliation problem. Ask for the booking reference in the note, in writing, next to the account number.
  • Some clients simply will not. Anyone booking on a phone at 11pm wants a card field. If the only option is a bank transfer, a share of them book somewhere else.

Deposit, card on file, and no-show fee are three different things

A deposit is money you hold. A card on file is authorisation to charge later, only as good as the consent wording. A no-show fee is a charge after the fact, and what you keep depends on where you trade. Do not blur them into one sentence: the no-show fee rules are not deposit rules. A written deposit policy stating amount, cancellation window and what happens to the money is worth more than any payment setting.

A setup that costs almost nothing

  1. Keep cards as the default for first-time clients. It converts and it protects the booking.
  2. Add a bank rail for repeat clients and anything over about $200, where the fixed fee starts to matter.
  3. Put the account details in the confirmation, with one line: send the deposit within 24 hours and reply with the reference, or the slot is released.
  4. Mark the appointment unconfirmed until the deposit clears. A cancellation window without a held deposit is a suggestion.

When to keep taking the card

If your average deposit is under $40, the fixed fee is small and a bank rail saves close to nothing. Mobile services, high-value one-offs and anything with refund risk want the card, because you want the dispute process on your side. A photographer taking a 30 percent retainer on an $1,800 shoot pays $50 once and should. A cleaning round taking $20 holds twelve times a week pays $150 a month for nothing.

The rule that matters

Pick the rail by what it protects, then count the fee. A deposit you cannot reconcile, or that a client cannot pay in ten seconds, costs more than the 3 percent you were trying to avoid.

Frequently asked

Can I pass card fees on to the client?

Sometimes, and it is usually a mistake. Surcharging is restricted or banned in several markets, and a visible processing fee reads as petty on a small deposit.

Is a bank transfer deposit as safe as a card deposit?

Safer against chargebacks, weaker on disputes and refunds. You keep the money unless you choose to return it, so the policy wording becomes the contract.

What percentage should a deposit be?

Enough to cover your cost of a late cancellation: usually 20 to 30 percent for time-based services and the full material cost for anything custom. Higher deposits cost you bookings; lower ones cost you Fridays.
