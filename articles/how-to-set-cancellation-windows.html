How to set cancellation windows: 24h vs 48h

A cancellation window is not a punishment. It is the amount of notice you need to resell the hour someone is giving back.

What the window actually protects

Most owners pick a cancellation window by copying the shop down the street, then wonder why nobody respects it. The number has one job: it must be long enough that you still have a realistic chance of filling the slot. That is a resale question, not a moral one.

Someone searching for a haircut on a Tuesday afternoon is usually looking at the next 24 to 72 hours. If your slot frees up two hours before it starts, the only taker is someone already on your waitlist who happens to be free and nearby. Two days ahead, the slot enters the normal booking flow and competes like any other.

24 hours versus 48 hours

Twenty-four hours is the industry default because it is easy to explain and clients accept it without argument. It suits city salons, barbershops and studios where demand is dense, clients are local, and a freed evening slot genuinely gets picked up by someone browsing that night.

Forty-eight hours buys you a second working day of selling time, which matters most when the service is long, expensive, or hard to staff at short notice. A three-hour colour, a mobile job with a travel window, a photoshoot with a second crew member, a reformer class with a waiting list that needs to be contacted one by one - all of these need a day to re-market, not an afternoon.

What changes is not client behaviour so much as recovery. Run your own arithmetic: 100 appointments a month, average ticket $80, 15 late cancellations. If 60% of slots cancelled with 48 hours' notice refill and only 25% of those cancelled inside 24 hours do, the longer window recovers nine appointments against under four - about $420 a month that the short policy never sees, usually more than fee revenue collects.

The four rules that make any window work

  1. Disclose it at booking, not at cancellation. The window belongs on the booking page, in the confirmation message and in the intake form footer. A policy a client first reads while cancelling is a policy they will fight.
  2. One clock for everyone. The moment you waive the window for a regular, the window stops existing for everyone who hears about it. If you want flexibility, build it into a stated goodwill allowance instead of an unwritten exception.
  3. Align it with your deposit. A window without a consequence is a suggestion. The cleanest pairing is a window plus a card on file or a deposit charged only when the window is missed - which keeps the policy credible without charging anyone who gives fair notice.
  4. Keep the window shorter than your rebooking horizon. If most clients book their next visit two weeks out, a 72-hour window is fine. If your average client rebooks within five days, a 72-hour window is theatre.

Matching the window to the business

  • Barbers, salons, nail studios: 24 hours. Demand is dense and local; late slots resell.
  • Mobile and at-home services: 48 hours. You are booking a travel window, fuel and a route, not just an hour.
  • High-ticket or multi-hour work (colour corrections, bridal, installations, event rentals): 72 hours to 7 days, with a deposit that scales to the lead time.
  • Group sessions and workshops: 48 hours for individuals, 7 days for private group bookings, because one cancellation removes several seats at once.

How to word it

Say what happens, not how disappointed you are. Three lines cover it: "Plans change - we get it. Cancel or move your appointment up to 48 hours before and there is no charge. Inside 48 hours we charge 50% of the service, because the slot rarely refills in time. Need to cancel? Reply to this message." The fee wording collection has longer variants for the booking page and the email footer, and the late cancellations guide covers the conversation when someone argues.

Then make the reminder carry the deadline, not just the time: "See you Thursday at 2pm. You can move this free of charge until Tuesday at 2pm." That single sentence does more for your calendar than any fee, because it turns a vague plan into a deadline people can act on.

Setting it up in software

Most tools call this a cancellation policy and let you pick hours before the appointment: Square Appointments, Booksy, Fresha and Calendly all do. Then test it from a client account: cancel at hour 47 and at hour 49 and see which one is charged. The settings page is not the policy - the client-facing flow is.

Track two numbers for a quarter: late cancellations per 100 bookings, and the share of freed slots that refilled. If refill share rises after you lengthen the window, keep it. If it does not, the problem is not the window - it is that nobody is being offered the empty slot, which is a reminder and waitlist problem. The mistake most shops make

They stretch the window to 72 hours, announce it, and then quietly stop enforcing it within a month because the conversation is uncomfortable. An enforced 24-hour window beats an ignored 72-hour one every time. Pick the shorter number you will actually charge, disclose it everywhere, and let the fee policy do the uncomfortable part for you.

Frequently asked

Is 24 or 48 hours better for a small salon?

24 hours is fine when demand is local and dense. Choose 48 if your services run two hours or more, or if freed slots rarely refill within a day.

Can I charge a fee for a cancellation inside the window?

Yes, if the window and the amount were disclosed before booking and the charge is proportional to the lost slot - commonly 50% of the service price. Check your local consumer rules for the upper limit.

Should regulars be exempt?

No exemptions, one stated goodwill waiver per client per year. Unwritten exceptions spread faster than the policy does.
