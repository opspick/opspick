Best CRM for small salons

Most salons do not need a separate CRM. They need the booking system they already pay for to remember four things about each client — and most of them only remember one.

The client record is the product

A booking system stores appointments. A CRM stores what makes the next appointment better: the colour formula, the allergy, the fact that she hates small talk, the fact that he always books the last slot on Friday. In a salon, that knowledge usually lives in one stylist's head, which is why losing a stylist hurts more than losing a chair.

So the question is not "which CRM should I buy" but "does my booking tool let me write this down, find it in ten seconds, and keep it when staff change". Four fields matter more than any dashboard:

  • Service history — what was done, by whom, how long it took.
  • Technical notes — formulas, timings, products, patch test dates.
  • Preferences — conversation, drinks, pressure, parking, running late.
  • Consent and contact — marketing permission, with a date, separate from booking consent.

The three tools, honestly

Fresha

The free core covers client profiles, history, notes, reminders and payments, which is more CRM than many small salons ever use. It is the easiest starting point if you have two to five chairs and no marketing budget. What it does not do is replace a real marketing tool: segments exist, but sophisticated lifecycle campaigns are limited.

Booksy

Built around the client app and the marketplace, so new-client acquisition is the strength. Profiles and notes are solid, and automated reminders and review requests are easy to switch on. The per-staff pricing is the thing to model — a six-chair salon pays for six, and that changes the maths against a flat-fee tool. Check current pricing before you commit.

Square

The best fit if your client data and your money should live in one place: Square's customer directory connects to payments, invoices, loyalty and marketing, and the reporting is genuinely useful. Salon-specific notes are thinner than in the two above, so a busy colourist may find the record field too shallow.

Notes only work if the habit exists

Every tool above gives you a text box. None of them will fill it. The difference between a salon with a useful CRM and one with an expensive address book is thirty seconds at the end of each appointment, and a rule that the note is written before the client reaches the door.

Make it concrete for staff: one line on the service, one line on anything the next person must know. No essays, no client gossip, nothing you would not want the client to read — because under most privacy regimes they can ask to see it. Client data privacy basics covers what belongs in a record and what does not.

Also decide who owns the data. If a stylist leaves, the notes stay with the salon, not with their personal account. Check that before you build a record you cannot export.

Marketing is where booking tools run out

Reactivation is the highest-return marketing a salon has: the client who came four times and stopped eight weeks ago. Most booking tools can send a reminder; fewer can build the segment "last visit 60–120 days ago, spent over a certain amount, opted in" and send something that is not a generic discount.

Two rules if you go further. Keep marketing consent separate from the booking terms, recorded with a date and a source. And keep the message small — a note that her usual stylist has a Tuesday slot converts better than a percentage off. Client reactivation campaigns has the sequence.

If you need real segmentation, email and SMS marketing on top of a booking tool is usually cheaper and better than switching everything to a CRM suite. Client communication tools compares the practical options.

What I would do

Small salon, one to four chairs, mostly regulars: Fresha for records and booking, with a strict note habit and a separate, simple way to email lapsed clients.

Salon that depends on new clients: Booksy, if the marketplace and reminders justify the per-staff cost — check current pricing against your chair count.

Salon already running retail and payments through Square: Square, for the single customer record, and accept that technical notes stay basic.

Only look at a dedicated salon CRM when you have multiple locations, commission reporting, or a marketing team. Below that, the tool you already have is the CRM — it just needs someone to write in it.
