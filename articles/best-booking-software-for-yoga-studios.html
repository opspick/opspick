Best booking software for yoga studios

A yoga studio does not sell appointments. It sells seats in a room at 7am, and the software has to know exactly how many seats there are.

A class is not an appointment

Most booking tools are built around one client, one practitioner, one slot. A studio is the opposite: one teacher, twenty mats, and the same twenty people coming back on a membership. If the software cannot put several people into one event, you will spend your week building the schedule by hand.

  1. Class capacity per event — a number, enforced, with a waitlist behind it.
  2. Waitlist that promotes automatically — and tells the person who moved up.
  3. Memberships and class packs — auto-renewing, plus credits that expire or do not.
  4. Drop-in payment — a card link that works on a phone at the door.
  5. Teacher substitution — change the teacher without rebuilding the class.

The three tools, honestly

Fresha

The free core now handles group classes, capacity and memberships, which makes it the easiest place for a small studio to start. Payments run through their processor, and the marketplace can bring in new students. If you teach three or four classes a week and mostly sell drop-ins and simple passes, this is enough.

Booksy

Good calendars, good reminders, per-staff pricing. Group classes are supported but the product clearly thinks in appointments first, so a full timetable with waitlists takes more setup. The per-staff fee applies to teachers, which is worth modelling before you commit a roster of six.

Square Appointments

Solid if the studio already runs retail and payments on Square. Class booking exists, but memberships and pack logic are less natural than in studio-specific tools. Choose it for the single dashboard, not for the class features.

Capacity, waitlists and the 7am problem

Set the capacity honestly. If the room fits eighteen mats with space to move, do not list twenty-two because the fire certificate allows it. Overbooking a yoga class is not like overbooking a haircut — people leave, and they tell the group chat.

The waitlist is the feature that pays for itself. When someone cancels at 10pm, the next person should be promoted automatically and told, with a link to claim the spot. If a human has to text three people in order, the seat stays empty and the teacher notices.

Decide your cancellation window and put it where people book, not in the terms page. Two hours is common for classes; whatever you choose, apply it consistently or the regulars will test it.

Memberships are a billing problem wearing a yoga mat

Most studio admin pain is not the timetable, it is the money: a monthly unlimited pass, a ten-class pack that expires in six months, a student rate, a founder price that must never change. Before you choose, write down every plan you sell and check that the tool can express it without a workaround.

Two details catch people out. First, whether credits expire and whether you can change that later. Second, what happens when a card fails — you want a retry and a clear message to the student, not a silent lapse.

What I would do

Small studio, a handful of weekly classes, mostly drop-ins and one membership: Fresha, capacity set honestly, waitlist on, cancellation window in the booking flow.

Studio with several teachers and a busy timetable: Booksy if reminders and a marketplace matter more than class-specific billing, or Square Appointments if you already sell retail and memberships through Square.

If your timetable grows past roughly thirty classes a week with multiple membership tiers, look at a studio-first platform instead — the general tools start to feel like a workaround, and the billing edge cases are where they break.
