How to handle no-show fees: what to charge and how to say it

A no-show fee is not punishment. It is the price of a chair nobody could use, written down before the booking so nobody has to argue about it afterwards.

What the fee is actually for

A 60-minute slot that empties at 10 minutes' notice costs you the same rent, the same staff hour and often the same prep. The fee exists to recover part of that loss and to make a repeat no-show expensive enough to stop being a habit. If your fee does neither - because it is unenforceable, or because you never actually charge it - it is decoration, and clients learn that within two weeks.

Choosing the number

Most operators land in one of three designs, and all three can work:

  • Percentage of the service. 50% is the common floor, 100% the ceiling. Percentage scales automatically when you raise prices, which is why it is the easiest to maintain.
  • Flat fee. $20-$50 per missed appointment, or one hour of your standard rate. Simple to explain at the door, but it under-charges your most expensive treatments.
  • Deposit that is kept. No separate fee at all: the client pays upfront and forfeits it. Easier to enforce because the money already moved, and there is nothing to chase.

The practical rule: your fee should cover the direct cost of the empty slot, not the profit you hoped to make. A fee that reads as a penalty invites chargebacks and angry reviews. A fee that reads as a shared cost gets paid without a conversation. If you are unsure which rail suits your clients, the comparison of deposits versus card on file is the decision to make first.

What makes it enforceable

Three things have to be true before you can charge anyone, and skipping any one of them turns a policy into a complaint:

  1. Disclosure before payment. The fee must appear on the booking page, in the confirmation message and in your terms. "By booking you agree" on a page nobody opens is not disclosure.
  2. Consent to the charge. Card on file needs an explicit tick, and the tick has to name the amount or the rule (for example "50% of the service price"). Storing a card silently and charging it later is how businesses end up in payment-processor disputes.
  3. Proportionality. Consumer rules in most of Europe and the UK, and in a growing number of US states, treat a charge that exceeds the actual loss as unfair. Keep a record of what the slot would have earned.

This is operational advice, not legal advice. If you take prepayments across borders, have a local adviser read the clause once - the wording is cheap to fix before a chargeback and expensive after.

The wording that gets paid without a fight

Put the fee in the same sentence as the cancellation window, so it reads as one rule rather than two threats:

"Life happens - you can move or cancel free up to 24 hours before your appointment. Inside 24 hours, or if we do not hear from you, we charge 50% of the service to cover the reserved time."

Then the reminder, 48 hours out: "See you Thursday at 2pm. Need to move it? You have until Wednesday at 2pm with no charge." The fee wording collection has variants for SMS, email and the booking page footer, and the reminder channel comparison explains which one actually gets replies.

When not to charge

Charging everyone every time is not discipline, it is churn. Skip the fee when the client called ahead, when it is a first offence by someone with a real history, or when the reason is an emergency you would also miss work for. Say so out loud: "No charge this time, I know it was short notice." That sentence buys more loyalty than the $30 would have.

Do charge consistently for the pattern that hurts: the third late cancellation, the client who books three slots "to decide later", the no-call-no-show. Consistency is what makes the policy credible - not severity.

Collecting it without drama

Charge the same day, through the same processor, with a one-line receipt note: "Late cancellation fee, 50% of 60-minute appointment." Do not chase by text for weeks; either the card on file works or you invoice once and move on. If you use a booking tool, set the fee up as an automatic rule rather than a manual decision:

  • Square Appointments - cancellation fee rules tied to the card on file.
  • Booksy - no-show and late-cancellation charges configured per service.
  • Fresha - deposit and cancellation policy attached at booking.
  • Acuity Scheduling - percentage or flat fee, with a client-facing policy page.

Whatever the tool, test it once with a friendly client before you rely on it. A rule that silently fails to charge is worse than no rule at all.

Measure the trade-off

Track three numbers monthly: no-shows per 100 bookings, fee revenue collected, and clients lost after a charge. If no-shows fall and fee revenue stays flat, the policy worked through deterrence. If clients vanish after the first charge, the fee is too high or the disclosure too quiet. Most shops find the sweet spot between 40% and 60% of the service price, disclosed at booking, charged automatically, with one goodwill waiver per client per year.

The rule that matters

Write the fee down before the booking, take the card at the booking, and charge it the same way every time. A policy you enforce unevenly costs more trust than the money it recovers.

Frequently asked

How much should a no-show fee be?

Enough to cover the direct cost of the empty slot - commonly 50% of the service price, or a flat fee equal to one hour of your standard rate. Above 100% it starts to look like a penalty and is easier to dispute.

Can I charge a client's card if they did not show up?

Only if the fee was disclosed before booking and the client explicitly agreed to the charge, with the amount or the rule named at that point. Silent card storage plus a surprise charge is the fastest route to a chargeback.

Should I charge a no-show fee to a regular client?

Waive the first one and say you are waiving it. Charge the pattern. Regulars who feel punished for one emergency quietly book elsewhere, and replacing them costs far more than the fee.
