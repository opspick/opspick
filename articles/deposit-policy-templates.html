Deposit policy templates and how to enforce them fairly

A deposit policy is mostly a wording problem. The version clients accept is short, shows the amount before they reach the card field, and is applied consistently enough that nobody feels singled out.

What a deposit actually does

A deposit does not stop people from forgetting. It changes the cost of forgetting, which is a different lever. Reminders handle absent-mindedness; a deposit handles the client who books three Saturdays "to decide later". Used together they work. Used alone, a deposit on a calendar with 30% empty slots mostly costs you bookings.

So the first question is not "how much" but "which appointments need protecting". In most small businesses that is a minority of the book: long services, peak hours, and first-time clients. Charge everybody and you tax your regulars for a problem they did not cause.

Choose the shape of the rule

There are four common shapes. Each has a failure mode worth knowing before you write the sentence.

RuleWorks well forWatch out for
Flat fee, e.g. $20Short services, walk-in-heavy booksFeels large on a cheap service
Percentage, e.g. 20–25%Colour, long treatments, high ticketAwkward to explain at the desk
Service-specificMixed books with one or two costly servicesClients ask why only some services
First visit onlyEstablished clientele, few no-showsRepeat offenders stay uncovered

Three templates you can copy

Template A — flat deposit, all bookings

Short enough to read on a phone, and it explains the refund instead of only the penalty:

"A $20 deposit is taken when you book and comes off your final bill. Cancel or move your appointment at least 24 hours ahead and it is refunded to the same card. Inside 24 hours, or if you don't arrive, the deposit is kept."

Two things make this work. It says the money comes off the bill, so the client reads it as prepayment rather than a fee. And it names the deadline in hours, not "24 hours' notice", which people misread as "any time the day before".

Template B — percentage for long services

"Services booked for two hours or more need a 25% deposit. That amount is deducted from your total. Reschedule at least 48 hours ahead for a full refund; after that the deposit covers the time we held."

Percentages scale with the loss. A cancelled four-hour colour costs you the same chair time whether the service was cheap or expensive, but the client's incentive only tracks the price if the deposit does too. Keep the percentage out of the headline and put the actual amount in the confirmation, so nobody has to do arithmetic at checkout.

Template C — first visit only

"First appointments are held with a $15 deposit, refunded if you cancel more than 24 hours ahead. After your first visit, no deposit is needed."

This is the gentlest version and the easiest to defend. It also gives you a clean answer for the client who asks why they are being charged: the rule is about the first appointment, not about them.

Enforcing it without losing people

The policy text is easy. The desk conversation is where businesses lose goodwill, usually by improvising. Decide the following in advance and write it where staff can see it:

  • One no-questions waiver per client, ever. Illness, a funeral, a cancelled flight — you cannot verify any of it, and arguing about it costs more than the deposit.
  • Never apply a new policy retroactively. Announce it, then start it on a stated date. Applying it to bookings made last week is the complaint that spreads.
  • Give each staff member a waiver allowance. One or two a month, no approval needed. Front desks that must escalate every exception start granting them silently instead.
  • Refund fast. A refund that arrives in two days ends the conversation. One that takes three weeks becomes a review.
  • Say the reason once. "It holds the slot for you" is enough. Long justifications sound like an apology and invite negotiation.
  • Avoid the wording that causes most complaints. Revealing the deposit at the payment step rather than the service step. Using "non-refundable" without saying what "refundable" would have been. Stacking a deposit with a separate late-cancellation fee, so one cancellation costs twice. And writing the rule in the plural — "clients are required to" — which reads like a policy aimed at the person reading it.

Where to configure it

Booksy, Fresha and Square Appointments all support deposits at booking with per-service rules; the details and fees differ, so check current pricing before you commit. If your current tool cannot take a card at the time of booking, the deposit policy exists only on paper, and clients learn that within a month.

Frequently asked

How much deposit should I take?

20% or a flat minimum, whichever is higher, for regular services. For weddings, events and 90-minute-plus appointments, 50% non-refundable is normal.

Where should the policy be visible?

On the booking page before payment, and repeated in the confirmation message — that is the message clients actually re-read.

Should I charge regulars a deposit?

No. Apply it to new clients and anyone with a prior no-show. Charging your best clients a deposit costs goodwill and prevents almost nothing.
