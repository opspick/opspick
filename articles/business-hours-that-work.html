Business hours that work for you, not just for the calendar

Most small service businesses inherit their hours from whoever opened the place, then defend them for years without checking whether the last two hours of the day pay for themselves.

Start from the booking data, not from habit

Before changing anything, export three months of appointments and count bookings by hour of day and day of week. You are looking for two shapes: the hours that fill reliably, and the hours that exist because you have always been open then.

What usually shows up: a strong late-afternoon and early-evening band, a soft mid-morning, and a Saturday that is either your best day or your worst depending on your client base. What rarely shows up is evidence that opening at 8am is necessary because one regular likes it.

  • Count bookings per hour, not revenue per day — averages hide the dead hours.
  • Separate new clients from returning ones; they behave differently.
  • Note the no-show rate per slot. Awkward hours are often the ones people book and then skip.
  • Mark the hours where staff cost exceeds the revenue that hour generates.

Price the quiet hours honestly

An hour that produces less than it costs is a choice, not a service. You may still keep it — for staff continuity, for client goodwill, for the walk-in trade — but keep it deliberately and write down why. Once it is written down, it is a decision you can review next quarter rather than a fact of nature.

Hour typeTypical patternOptions
PeakFills early, waitlist formsProtect it; consider a small premium
SteadyMostly full, some gapsLeave alone
QuietHalf empty most weeksDiscount, or shorten the day
DeadRegularly emptyClose it, or use it for admin and training

A common compromise is to keep the door closed but the room in use: quiet hours become the time for stock, cleaning, training and admin, which otherwise gets done after closing and is never paid for.

Match hours to how clients actually book

Your opening hours and your booking hours are not the same thing. Many businesses take online bookings around the clock and answer the phone for four hours a day. Saying so plainly reduces frustration on both sides: "Book online any time. Phone answered 9–12 and 2–5."

If you use a booking tool, set its availability windows to match reality rather than aspiration. A slot that appears bookable and then gets moved is worse than a slot that never appeared. Protect the edges of the day

The first and last appointment of the day carry disproportionate risk. A late-running last client costs you closing time and staff patience; an early first client means the room must be ready before anyone has had a coffee. Two small rules help:

  1. Do not book your longest service as the final appointment of the day unless the practitioner chooses it.
  2. Leave a fixed buffer before closing — 15 to 30 minutes — that is not bookable at all.
  3. Set the last bookable start time explicitly, rather than letting the system fill to the closing minute.

These are the settings most often missed during setup, and they are the ones that cause the arguments about running over.

Change them rarely, then review twice a year

Hours that shift every few weeks teach clients not to rely on them. When you do change, give more notice than feels necessary — four to six weeks for a reduction — and tell the affected clients directly, not only through a social post. The people who will be inconvenienced are the ones who never see your feed.

Update every place the hours appear: the site, the booking widget, Google Business Profile, the door sign, the voicemail greeting and the automatic confirmation message. Stale hours are one of the most common causes of a one-star review, and the fix costs ten minutes. Staff scheduling for small salons covers the internal side of the same problem.

A simple review rhythm

Look at hours twice a year, in the same months, using the same report. Ask three questions: which hour made the least money, which hour caused the most stress, and which client would we lose if we closed it. If the answer to the last one is "nobody we would miss", you have your next change.

There is no ideal schedule. There is only the one that pays for the room, keeps the team able to work well, and does not require you to be open when nobody comes.
