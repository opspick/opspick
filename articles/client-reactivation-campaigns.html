Client reactivation campaigns that don't feel desperate

Most lapsed clients did not leave because of a complaint. They drifted, and a message that assumes they are angry or forgetful is the one that keeps them away.

Work out who actually lapsed

You cannot reactivate a list. You reactivate individuals, and the definition of lapsed depends on the service. A client who comes every three weeks is overdue at eight; a client on a six-month maintenance schedule is not overdue at four. Set the threshold per service, not per business.

A rough rule that travels well: lapsed means more than twice the normal return interval. Inactive means more than three times. Those are different campaigns with different tone, and merging them is why so many "we miss you" blasts get ignored.

SegmentDefinitionBest first move
Due soon80–100% of normal intervalNormal reminder, no incentive
Lapsed2–3× normal intervalPersonal note, easy rebook link
Inactive3× interval or 6+ monthsOne low-pressure message, then stop
Never returnedOne visit, no second bookingSeparate onboarding follow-up

Also exclude anyone with an open complaint, an unpaid balance, or a request not to be contacted.

Lead with usefulness, not with a discount

The instinct is to open with a percentage off. It works sometimes, but it also teaches clients that waiting is rewarded, and it attracts the people least likely to stay. Better openings are specific and true:

  • A note about something that changed since their last visit — new hours, a new practitioner, a treatment that now exists.
  • A practical reminder tied to the service: "your last colour was in March, and regrowth usually shows around week six."
  • A direct question: "are you still seeing us elsewhere, or did we get something wrong?" That one gets replies, including honest ones.

Discounts are not forbidden. They just work better in the second message than the first, and better still as a time-limited reason ("three slots this week") than as a standing price cut.

Keep the ask small

Asking a client who has not visited in five months to book a two-hour appointment is a large ask. Asking them to reply with one word is small. A single-question message — "still interested, or should I stop emailing?" — routinely gets more responses than a polished campaign, because it is easy to answer.

When someone does reply positively, book them while the conversation is open. Sending a booking link and waiting is where most reactivation dies. If you can put the slot in the reply yourself, do that.

Pick the channel the client used

Email is cheap and easy to ignore. SMS gets read and feels intrusive if it is unexpected. A quick call converts best and scales worst. Match the channel to the history: if they always booked by text, text them; if they came through your online booking, email is fine. SMS reminder tools and the WhatsApp comparison cover the delivery side if you are setting this up for the first time. Booksy and Fresha include campaign messaging on paid plans; check current pricing.

Whatever the channel, include an easy way to say no. "Reply STOP" is the legal minimum in many places and the courteous one everywhere. Honouring opt-outs immediately is not optional.

Run it as a short sequence, then stop

Three touches over three to four weeks is plenty: a personal note, a practical reminder with a specific opening, and a final short "should I close your file?" message. After that, stop and move them to a lower-frequency list. Long campaigns feel like pressure and cost you the ability to contact them again later.

Measure two things: how many replied, and how many booked. Reply rate tells you whether the message landed; booking rate tells you whether the offer was any good. If replies are high and bookings are low, the problem is the ask or the times you offered, not the copy.

Fix the reason they drifted

Reactivation is a repair job, and repairs are cheaper than replacements. If most lapsed clients stopped after a long wait, a price change, or a scheduling clash, the campaign will bring them back into the same problem. Look at the exit reasons before you spend on the message.

Two changes do most of the work: making rebooking automatic at the end of an appointment, covered in client rebooking automation, and keeping reminder timing sensible. A client who leaves with their next date already booked does not need to be reactivated at all.

The honest summary: reactivation works when it is small, specific, and short. It fails when it is a mass email with a coupon, sent quarterly, to a list nobody has cleaned in two years.
