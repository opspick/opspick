How to price mobile services: travel fees without losing clients

A travel fee is not a surcharge on your service. It is the price of a different service, delivered somewhere else. Price it that way and nobody argues.

The mistake almost everyone makes first

The first version of most mobile price lists is one radius and one flat fee: "20 miles, $20 extra." It is wrong at both ends. A client eight minutes away pays the same as one across a bridge in traffic, so the near client subsidises the far one. And when a far booking eats ninety minutes for sixty minutes of paid work, the flat fee has covered nothing.

Price the zone, not the mile

Base tiers on drive time from your usual start, not distance. Ten miles of motorway and ten miles of city traffic are not the same product. Three zones cover almost every one-person operation:

  • Zone A, under 15 minutes. Travel included. This should be most of your week.
  • Zone B, 15 to 30 minutes. $15 to $30 as a visible line item. Not a profit centre, just an honest recovery.
  • Zone C, 30 to 45 minutes. $35 to $50, a higher minimum booking value, and only on days you are already going that way.
  • Beyond that. Quote only. No published price, because you will resend it three times and still lose the booking.

Set the boundaries with a stopwatch, not a map: drive to two or three real clients at the times you would serve them, noting door-to-door minutes including parking and setup.

Make the fee visible before the client picks a time

A travel fee discovered at checkout is a complaint. One seen while choosing a slot is a decision, and clients accept decisions. Most booking tools do this without a custom build: create the service as variants per zone, or add a travel line item selected during booking. Square Appointments handles service variants cleanly, and Booksy and Fresha support priced add-ons that work as a travel charge. If yours cannot, put the zone table on the booking page and ask the zone first.

Because travel is priced by area, the shape of your week matters as much as the fee. Clustering days by area - "north side Tuesdays, south side Thursdays" - cut forty minutes of daily driving for one mobile beauty business.

Set a minimum, not a discount

The rule that keeps mobile work profitable is a minimum job value per zone, not a bigger travel fee. If a Zone C round trip costs ninety minutes, a $45 service cannot carry it. Publish "Zone C bookings start at $120" and small jobs self-select out before they reach you. Sell it as a bundle, not a penalty.

Waive it once, deliberately

A one-time travel waiver is the strongest closing tool a mobile business has, precisely because the fee was visible first. Say the number, then remove it for the first visit: the client sees what the service is worth and feels the concession. If you never show the fee, the waiver costs money and buys nothing. Do not extend it to a second visit "because it went well" - that is how a fee becomes a permanent discount.

Protect the travel you have already spent

Mobile work is exposed in a way shop work is not: by the time you learn the client is not home, you have paid the fuel and the hour. Take a card on file or a small deposit for Zone B and C bookings. Then make the late cancellation fee cover travel as well as time, in writing: "Cancellations within 24 hours are charged the travel fee plus 50%" is a receipt for something you paid. "50% of the service" invites an argument.

Scripts that survive the conversation

On the phone: "Where are you based? That puts you in Zone B, so the visit is $25 on top, and I am in your area on Thursdays." Zone, number, day. When they push back: "The fee covers the drive and the setup, not the treatment. If you come to me, or a neighbour books the same afternoon, it comes off." Two alternatives beat one discount.

The number to check every month

Track revenue per hour of driving, not per client: add driving time to service time, total the money, divide. If it sits below what you would earn in a shop, your zones are wrong or you are accepting bookings you should decline. Most mobile businesses find the culprit is one postcode area supplying a fifth of the bookings and half of the unpaid hours.

Keep mileage records too. The standard rate is a real deduction, and the current figure is at irs.gov.

Frequently asked

Should I charge a travel fee or build it into the price?

Build it into Zone A only. Everywhere else, show it as a separate line so the client sees what the drive costs and can choose to avoid it. Hidden travel costs make your service look expensive next to a shop with none.

What is a fair travel fee for a mobile service?

Roughly the value of the time, not the fuel: $15 to $30 for a 15 to 30 minute drive, $35 to $50 for 30 to 45 minutes. The hour you cannot sell is the real cost.
