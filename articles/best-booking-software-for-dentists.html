Best booking software for dentists

A dental practice does not really sell appointments. It sells a chair, a clinician, and a recall that has to happen again in six months whether or not the patient remembers.

A recall is not an appointment

General booking tools assume the customer decides when to come back. Dentistry assumes the opposite: the practice decides, and the patient is invited. If the software cannot hold a recall interval on a patient record and generate the invitation, someone at the front desk is doing it by memory.

  • Recall intervals per patient — six months for one, three for another, with a pause for those who moved away.
  • Multiple chairs and providers — two hygienists working in parallel without double-booking the room.
  • Appointment types with different lengths — a check-up, a filling, a crown prep.
  • Medical history forms — filled in before arrival, stored with the record.
  • A note on the booking — so the clinician knows why the patient is coming.

The three tools, honestly

Fresha

The free core is genuinely capable for a small practice: calendars, client records, reminders, card payments. What it is not is a clinical system. There is no dental chart, no treatment plan, no insurance handling. It works as a front-of-house diary and nothing more.

Booksy

Similar story with better reminders and a stronger consumer marketplace. Per-staff pricing is the thing to model carefully — a practice with four clinicians and two hygienists pays for six seats, and that number grows every time you hire.

Square Appointments

The most sensible of the three if you already take payments on Square. Multi-location support is decent and the reporting is clear. Still a general tool: recalls and clinical notes live outside it.

Chairs, not calendars

The scheduling unit in a practice is the chair and the person in it. A hygiene appointment blocks a room for forty minutes; an emergency extraction may need the same room for an hour with a dentist free at the same time. Software that only understands "staff member is busy" will let you promise a room you do not have.

Test this before you buy: put two providers on the same chair at the same time and see whether the tool stops you. If it does not, the front desk becomes the collision detector, and the front desk is already busy.

The second thing to test is the gap. A cancelled 9:20 appointment leaves a hole that is too short for a crown and too long to leave empty. Good tools let you see the gap and offer it to a waiting list; weak ones just show a blank square.

Deposits, forms and the awkward conversations

Dental no-shows are expensive because the slot was worth real money. A deposit or a card on file changes behaviour more than a reminder text does, but it has to be explained at booking, not sprung at the door. Decide the rule, write it in one sentence, and put it where the patient clicks.

Forms are the other quiet win. A medical history completed at home is more accurate than one filled in on a clipboard while a child waits. Check that answers land attached to the patient record rather than in an inbox someone has to file.

What I would do

A single-chair practice, mostly private work, no clinical software yet: start with Square Appointments if payments already run through Square, or Fresha if you want the free tier and a marketplace listing. Keep clinical notes in whatever you use today.

A growing practice with hygienists, recalls and insurance: use a dental-specific practice management system for the clinical side, and treat these tools as the online booking layer only if they integrate. Do not try to make a general booking tool hold a treatment plan — Booksy will schedule the visit beautifully and remember nothing about the tooth.

Whatever you pick, check current pricing for the number of providers you actually have, not the number you have today.
