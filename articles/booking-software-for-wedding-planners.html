Best booking software for wedding planners: vendor calls, trials and the day-of timeline

A wedding planner's calendar holds three different kinds of time: meetings with the couple, calls with suppliers, and one day that cannot move. Most booking tools only understand the first one.

Why a normal appointment calendar runs out of room

A salon books one person into one slot. A planner books a couple into a discovery call, then a venue walkthrough, then a hair and makeup trial, then a tasting, and in between schedules phone calls with a florist, a band and a caterer who all have to agree on the same hour in the same week.

Book that in a plain calendar and the failures arrive in order: the inquiry call is booked before you know the budget, the trial gets moved and nobody tells the makeup artist, and the day-of timeline lives in three documents that disagree.

The seven features that decide it

  1. An inquiry form that filters. Date, guest count, budget band and venue status. Two dates in the same month that are already taken should stop the inquiry, not surprise you on the call.
  2. One record per wedding. Couple, vendors, payments, files and dates in a single place, so a rescheduled call moves with the event.
  3. Proposal, contract and deposit in one flow. The client signs and pays from the same link, and the booking only confirms when the deposit clears.
  4. Vendor call slots with buffers. Fifteen minutes of padding between calls, because the florist always runs over.
  5. Appointments owned by a third party. Trials, tastings and fittings belong to the venue or the artist, not to you. You need to book them and still see them.
  6. A shareable run-of-show. One timeline, exported per vendor, versioned and dated.
  7. Payment schedules. Several instalments per contract, with reminders that fire before the date, not after.

The tools worth looking at

HoneyBook is the common starting point: inquiry form, proposal, e-signed contract, invoice and a client portal in one thread. Not a wedding tool, so questionnaires stay generic, but the money side is finished.

Dubsado does the same work with deeper automation: branching forms and workflows that trigger on a signed contract. More setup, less hand-holding.

Aisle Planner is built for this job specifically: checklists, vendor management, seating, budget tracking and a day-of timeline your suppliers can actually open. It is weak on the sales pipeline, so most planners pair it with a CRM.

Planning Pod covers events beyond weddings, with floor plans and banquet sheets. Worth it if you also run corporate work.

For the calls themselves, Calendly or Cal.com in front of the CRM beats most built-in schedulers. See where a plain calendar stops working.

Vendor calls: the part nobody schedules properly

Suppliers answer in batches, at odd hours, and reschedule twice. Block a two-hour window per wedding per week labelled "vendor calls", then book confirmed calls inside it. A no-show then costs fifteen minutes, not an afternoon.

Put the quote in the same record as the call. A verbal price from a florist in March is the number everyone argues about in June, and the quote-to-deposit flow is where that argument is usually lost.

Trials, tastings and fittings

These are appointments you arrange but do not host, which breaks most booking tools. Three rules: the vendor's address goes on the confirmation, a note field records what was decided, and the slot is re-checked whenever the date moves. A trial that quietly stays in the old week is the most common self-inflicted problem in the planning cycle.

The couple reschedules too; the buffer rules in our last-minute bookings guide apply unchanged.

Deposits and instalments

Retainer, a lump sum, then the balance before the day. If the tool cannot schedule three instalments against one contract with reminders, you will chase them by hand every month. Set the wording once from the deposit policy templates and send the intake form with the confirmation so allergies and addresses arrive before the first design meeting.

The day-of timeline is the product

Couples do not recommend you for the spreadsheet. They recommend you because the day ran on time. One timeline, one owner, a version number that changes when anything changes, and a per-vendor export: the caterer sees service times, the band sees the first dance, the photographer sees the light. A tool that only prints one internal PDF leaves you rebuilding it by hand the week before.

What to ignore while choosing

  • Hour tracking. You sell a flat fee, not time.
  • Marketing suites. Couples choose on referrals and portfolio, not email drip campaigns.
  • Seating charts in the booking tool. Nice demo, never the reason a wedding goes well.

A realistic first month

Week one: inquiry form and date rules. Week two: proposal, contract and deposit. Week three: vendor records and call windows. Week four: the master timeline, exported once to prove it works. Move existing couples at a quiet moment, following the migration checklist order.

The month-end test: a couple books a call, signs, pays the retainer, and the florist gets the right date without you forwarding anything. If that works, the software is doing the job.

Frequently asked

Can a wedding planner run on Calendly and a spreadsheet?

For the first five weddings, yes. The moment you take two deposits a month the spreadsheet loses track of instalments, and the day-of timeline becomes a document three people edit badly.

Do I need both a CRM and a wedding planning tool?

Most solo planners do: a HoneyBook or Dubsado for contracts and money, and Aisle Planner for the timeline and vendor detail. Full-service teams with 20+ weddings a year often consolidate into one.

How far ahead should the final timeline be locked?

Two weeks before the date, with changes after that logged as version two and sent to every vendor. Anything later than 72 hours is a phone call, not an email.
