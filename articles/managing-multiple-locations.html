Managing multiple locations: clients, staff and stock across sites

A second site does not double your revenue problems — it multiplies the small ones. Three things break first: client history, staff cover and stock count.

Clients belong to the business, not the building

The most expensive mistake new multi-site owners make is treating each location as its own client list. A client who books at site A and then walks into site B gets asked the same intake questions twice, sits through a first-visit slot she does not need, and often is charged a different price. She notices every one of those things.

One record per person, shared across sites. Notes, allergies, preferred stylist, deposit history and no-show count should follow the client, not the address. If your booking tool is per-location, at minimum agree on one client identifier — usually the mobile number — and make staff look it up before creating a new file. If the software cannot do this at all, that is a substitute-it problem; the vendor comparison in booking software for multi-location businesses covers which tools share records properly.

Shared records raise one question you should answer in writing before you open: which site owns the client for marketing purposes? Pick a rule — nearest site, or the site of the last visit — and stick to it, or both sites will email the same person twice a month and blame each other.

Staff: rotations, not transfers

Clients follow people. If your best stylist works Tuesday at one site and Thursday at the other, half her clients will show up at the wrong door in the first month. The fix is boring and it works: publish each person's site schedule a month ahead, put it on the booking page per location, and have reception confirm the site in the confirmation message.

Two rules keep rotation from destroying utilisation:

  • Minimum two days per site per person. One-day stints mean the commute eats the margin and nobody learns the local clientele.
  • No solo coverage in the first 90 days of a new site. Two staff at all times, so a single no-show does not empty the room.

Pay and commission need a decision too. Charging one site's rent against another site's payroll hides a failing branch for a year. Track per-person revenue by site; the general schedule mechanics are in staff scheduling for small salons.

Inventory moves faster than you think

Retail and back-bar stock will migrate. Products get borrowed for a colour service, a client's favourite shampoo is out at one site, someone "just grabs it" from the other. Without a transfer log you learn about it at stocktake, when the numbers no longer reconcile and nobody remembers.

Keep a one-page transfer sheet — item, quantity, from, to, who, date — and reconcile it weekly, not monthly. Count high-value items weekly, everything else monthly. If a product is stocked at both sites, keep the par levels visible so nobody over-orders to feel safe; the shrinkage and counting routine in inventory tracking for salons applies unchanged with one extra column for site.

Price once, publish everywhere

Different prices for the same service at two sites is defensible if the rent differs and the client can see why. It is indefensible when the difference is a forgotten menu update. Update prices in one place, on one date, and reprint or re-publish every menu the same week. Announce increases with notice, exactly as you would for a single site — the wording in raising prices without losing clients holds, and the peak/quiet week logic in seasonal pricing strategies can differ by site as long as each site's page states its own peak dates.

Report per site, decide per site

One combined dashboard flatters a weak branch. Four numbers per location, weekly: bookings per available hour, average ticket, retail attach rate, and repeat rate at 60 days. A site that is fine on revenue but poor on utilisation is overstaffed; the reverse is a capacity problem. Act on the branch, not the average.

Access and privacy

Shared records mean every employee can see every client's history, including the other site's. Give staff access to the records they need for their own bookings, keep card data out of notes, and write down who can export a client list and why. The baseline is in client data privacy basics.

The 30-day checklist before opening site two

  1. Client records shared, or a lookup rule agreed and posted at reception.
  2. Each person's site schedule published a month ahead.
  3. Transfer log live, weekly reconciliation booked in the calendar.
  4. One price list, one update date, every menu republished.
  5. Per-site weekly report with the same four numbers.
  6. Loyalty points and gift cards valid at both sites — clients will assume they are.

That last point is a quiet revenue leak. Gift cards sold at one site and refused at the other cost you the client, not just the balance; see selling gift cards for the terms wording.

What software actually has to do

You need shared client records, per-location calendars, staff-level permissions and per-site reporting. Square Appointments and Vagaro both handle multiple locations on their paid tiers; Booksy and Fresha are workable at two sites but thinner on consolidated stock. Check the per-location fee before you commit — it is the number that decides whether site two pays for itself in month three or month ten.

Frequently asked

Should each location have its own price list?

Only when rent or service cost genuinely differs, and only with a visible reason. Accidental differences from forgotten menu updates cost trust.

How do I stop clients booking the wrong site?

Show the site in the confirmation message, publish each staff member's site schedule a month ahead, and confirm the address on the reminder 24 hours out.
