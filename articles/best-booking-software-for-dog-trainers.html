Best booking software for dog trainers: packages, behaviour notes and owner handover

A dog trainer does not sell one-hour appointments. They sell a six-session package, a group class, and a written handover the owner will actually read. Most booking tools only handle the first hour.

What a trainer actually schedules

Four different products live in one calendar, and they behave nothing alike:

  • One-to-one sessions sold in blocks of four to eight, paid upfront, used over six to ten weeks. The client needs to see how many sessions are left without texting you.
  • Group classes with a hard cap of six to eight dogs, a fixed start date, and a waiting list that fills the next cohort.
  • Behaviour consultations at 90 to 120 minutes, often at the client's home, usually before any package is sold.
  • Follow-ups and check-ins that are not visits at all — a 15-minute call or a video review of a training clip.

Generic calendar tools handle the third item well and the other three badly. That mismatch is where trainers end up back on a spreadsheet of session credits, which is exactly the admin load the software was supposed to remove.

The five things that matter

  1. Session credits, visible to the client. The client should open a link and see "3 of 6 remaining, expires 14 Feb". Anything less generates a message every week.
  2. Capacity limits on classes. A hard cap plus a waiting list, not a note in the description asking people to be reasonable.
  3. A client record that holds behaviour notes. Not just name and phone: dog name, breed, age, triggers, what worked last session. Free-text notes are enough; structured fields become a chore.
  4. Deposits or prepayment on the first booking. No-shows in training are expensive because the slot cannot be filled at short notice.
  5. An owner handover you can send. A summary email or PDF after each session, so the person doing the homework knows what it is.

The short list

Time To Pet — the best fit for most solo trainers

Time To Pet is built for pet care businesses: visits, packages, client app, sitter or trainer notes, and photos sent to the owner. Pricing starts around $45/month for a solo operator, which is more than a generic scheduler but replaces three tools. Its scheduling model assumes visits at a client's address, so group classes need manual setup and a capacity rule you enforce yourself.

Acuity Scheduling — the strongest package and class-series engine

Acuity sells packages and class series natively: a client buys six sessions, books them from a private link, and the balance decrements automatically. Intake forms attach to the appointment, and the class calendar enforces capacity with a waiting list. The interface is dated and the client-facing booking flow is wordier than it should be, but nothing on this list handles credits more cleanly. Plans start around $16/month.

Square Appointments — free for a solo trainer, and enough to start

Square Appointments is free for one person, takes card payments and deposits in the same flow, and attaches intake forms to bookings. There is no real package credit tracking — you sell a $600 "six-session pack" as an item and track usage in the client note. For a trainer under 20 sessions a week that is a fair trade for zero monthly cost; past that, the manual tracking becomes the bottleneck.

SimplyBook.me — group classes without paying for a full suite

SimplyBook.me handles class capacity, recurring sessions and waiting lists well, and its free tier covers 50 bookings a month. The trade-off is that features are sold as add-ons, so the price climbs once you need SMS reminders and multiple staff. Good for a trainer whose main product is a weekly puppy class.

Gingr or DaySmart Pet — if training sits inside a daycare

If you also board or daycare dogs, a pet-care suite beats a trainer tool. Gingr and DaySmart Pet both handle vaccination records, owner portals and package billing, and both cost real money — roughly $100 to $200/month. Only worth it when the training arm shares clients with the daycare.

Where each one breaks in month two

  • Square: multi-dog households. Two dogs, one owner, and the package credits blur. You will end up with two client records and no shared history.
  • Acuity: behaviour notes. Notes live on the appointment, not the dog, so a six-session history is scattered across six bookings.
  • Time To Pet: group classes. Capacity is not enforced, so the seventh dog books into a six-dog class and you refund by hand.
  • SimplyBook.me: the per-feature pricing. The $30 plan becomes $60 once reminders, staff and class modules are on.

None of these are dealbreakers — but pick the one whose weak spot you can live with, not the one with the longest feature list.

Behaviour notes and the handover

This is the part no booking tool sells you well, and it is the part clients remember. Keep one running note per dog with four headings: triggers, what worked, what to practise, next session's goal. Write it in the ten minutes after the session, not the night before the next one. Then send a two-paragraph summary to the owner the same day — an email template is enough, and it doubles as proof of progress when they hesitate about renewing the package.

Renewal is where trainers leak money. A package that ends without a booked next session is a client who "will think about it". Set the sixth session to prompt a rebooking request automatically, and price the next block at the same rate for 14 days. That one rule recovers more revenue than any scheduling feature.

What to set up first

  1. One service per product: consultation, single session, six-session package, group class. Do not create twenty variations.
  2. A deposit on the consultation only. Once they have met you, prepayment for the package is normal.
  3. Two reminders: 48 hours and 3 hours. Anything more trains clients to ignore them.
  4. A cancellation window of 24 hours, stated on the booking page, not just in the terms.

If you are still choosing between a trainer-specific tool and a general scheduler, the deciding question is simple: do you sell packages or single sessions? Packages point to Acuity or Time To Pet. Single sessions point to Square. Everything else is detail you can change later — and if you do change, follow a migration checklist rather than rebuilding the calendar from memory.

Frequently asked

What is the cheapest option for a new dog trainer?

Square Appointments, free for one person. You give up package credit tracking but you get deposits, intake forms and reminders in one place, which is enough for the first 20 sessions a week.

Do I need trainer-specific software?

Only if you run group classes or sell session blocks. A general scheduler handles single appointments fine; packages and capacity are where generic tools fall apart.

How should I charge for a six-session package?

Upfront, with a 14-day window to rebook the next block at the same price. Per-session billing invites cancellations at exactly the point where the dog is starting to improve.
