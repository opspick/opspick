Deposits with cash clients: when the client refuses a card

No card, no hold, no protection. The deposit still works - it just has to be built differently.

Every deposit policy assumes a card on file. Then a regular walks in, says "I only do cash, I don't give my card to apps", and the policy hits a wall. You have two bad options: insist and lose the booking, or waive it and teach the client list that deposits are optional. There is a third path, and it is mostly about picking the right holding mechanism.

A card deposit does two jobs at once: it secures money and it creates a record that survives a no-show. Cash only does the first, and only while you are physically holding it. That difference matters, because most no-show damage comes from the missing record, not the missing money.

So the goal with a cash client is not "get the cash". It is "create a traceable commitment". Four mechanisms do that, and they are not equally good.

The four workable options

OptionProtectionEffortBest for
Cash taken in person at bookingHigh - you hold itHigh - client visits twiceNeighbourhood shops, walk-in culture
Tap-to-pay on your phoneHigh - same as cardLow once set upMobile operators, home visits
Prepaid credit or gift cardMedium - balance can lapseMedium - needs a ledgerRegulars who book months ahead
Written commitment, no moneyLow - social onlyNoneLong-standing regulars only

The first two are real deposits. The third is a deposit with an expiry problem. The fourth is not a deposit at all, and only makes sense for someone who has already shown up ten times.

Cash in hand, or a tap on your phone

Cash at booking works and filters hard. The client pays in person, you write a receipt, the amount comes off the final bill. The cost is friction: you are asking for a second visit before the first one. Keep it for local clients who pass the door anyway.

Two rules keep it clean. Record the cash against the specific appointment, not against the client, or you will be guessing at checkout. And hand over something printed - even a slip with date, amount and appointment time. The receipt is the whole protection.

Tap-to-pay is the closest thing to a card deposit for a cash-first client, because they tap their own card in front of you. They are not "giving a card to an app"; they are paying, like buying coffee. The objection usually disappears there. A phone with tap-to-pay enabled, or a reader attached to it, handles this: Square is the common choice, and Booksy and Fresha both let you record a deposit against an appointment, so the balance is already reduced when the client arrives. Check current hardware and processing terms on each provider's site - those change.

Prepaid credit needs an expiry

The client hands you cash for a credit on their account, spent against future appointments. The trap is bookkeeping: a balance with no expiry becomes an open-ended liability you can never close.

State a clear expiry window in the terms, in writing at the time of payment, and note it on the client record. If your booking tool cannot hold a client balance, skip this option - a spreadsheet you forget to update is worse than no deposit.

What to say, and how to record it

  • On the phone: "The slot is held with a deposit - it comes off your bill. I can take cash at the shop, or you can tap a card when you arrive."
  • If they refuse both: "Then I will put you on the waitlist for that day and text you if it frees up." No exception.
  • For a long-standing regular: "You have never missed one, so I am not asking. This is for new bookings." Say it out loud so the next person hears the rule exists.

The mistake is negotiating. A rule that bends under mild pressure gets tested every week. Whatever route you use, the deposit must exist in three places: on the appointment, in the payment record, and on the client's receipt. Deduct it before you quote the total at checkout - "that is the balance, your deposit is already off" - rather than after they hand over cash. Write the cash rule into the same deposit policy as the card rule, with the mechanism named; "deposit required" alone is the version that gets argued with.

The short version

Take cash in person or tap a card on your phone. Treat prepaid credit as a balance with an expiry, not a deposit. Skip the handshake exception except for regulars who have earned it, and always leave a trace - the trace is what stops the no-show, not the note in your pocket.

Frequently asked

Can I refuse a booking if the client will not pay a deposit?

Yes, and doing it consistently is what makes the policy credible. Offer the waitlist instead of an exception.

Is a cash deposit still a deposit without a receipt?
