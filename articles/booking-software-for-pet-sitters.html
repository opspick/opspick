Booking software for pet sitters: visits, keys and emergency contacts

A pet sitting booking is not one appointment. It is twelve visits, two keys, a medication schedule and a phone number that has to work at 7am on a Sunday.

Why a salon calendar does not fit

Most booking tools model one client, one slot, one staff member. Pet sitting breaks all three assumptions. A single booking covers several visits per day, spread across a week, often split between two sitters who never overlap. The client is not in the room. And the thing that matters most — the key — has no field in a salon app, so it ends up in a spreadsheet or a group chat, which is exactly where it should not be.

That is why the honest answer for most sitters is a pet-specific tool rather than the cheapest appointment app. The gap is not scheduling. The gap is records.

The four things the software has to hold

  1. Multi-visit bookings. "Three visits a day, Monday to Friday" must be one booking with one price, not fifteen bookings the client has to approve individually. Editing the whole series at once is the test; trip changes are normal, not exceptional.
  2. Key and access records. Key tag number, lockbox code, alarm code, garage code, where the spare is. Attached to the client, visible only to the sitter assigned to that visit, and revocable the day the client leaves.
  3. Pet files. Food amount, medication and timing, allergies, vet practice, emergency contact with a second number, and a signed authorization to treat up to a spending cap.
  4. Visit reports. A photo, a note, a timestamp. This is the product clients actually feel they paid for, and it is also your defence if anyone questions whether you showed up.

Keys are the liability, not the calendar

Every sitter has a key story. The software cannot fix the story, but it can make the paperwork boring. Four rules worth enforcing:

  • Log every key movement. Collected, copied, returned, lost. A timestamp and a name, in the client record, not in a message thread.
  • Never photograph a key next to its address. Obvious, and still the most common leak when a phone gets stolen.
  • Return keys on the last visit of the last booking, and say so in the confirmation. Clients forget they gave you one; you cannot.
  • Charge a rekeying fee if a key is lost, and put the number in the terms. A stated fee turns a crisis into a policy.

Store codes in the client record only if the tool encrypts them and limits access by role. If it does not, keep the alarm code with the client via a password manager share and record only "code on file" in the app.

The emergency protocol has to be readable at 6am

Emergency information fails when it is buried. The sitter has a sick dog, one free hand and no time to search. So the record needs three things on one screen: the vet's name and number, the client's second contact, and the authorization line with the spending cap. Anything more than a tap away is decoration.

Collect that on the intake form at signup, not the night before the trip. The selection checklist covers the rest of the evaluation. Reminders matter here too: a client who forgets to leave the food out is the pet sitting version of a no-show.

What I would do

Solo, under 30 regular clients: PetSitClick, and spend the saved subscription on a proper lockbox for each client instead of holding keys.

Two to five sitters: Precise Petcare, with the key log enforced from day one.

Growing team with staff turnover: Time To Pet. You are paying for role-based access as much as scheduling — the part that stops a departing sitter taking your client list with them.

Frequently asked

Can I just use Square or Booksy for pet sitting?

For walks with fixed slots, yes. For trips with multiple daily visits, key records and per-visit reports, no — you will end up with the schedule in the app and everything important in a spreadsheet.

Should I hold client keys or use lockboxes?

Lockboxes for most clients. Holding keys is fine for long-term regulars if you log every movement and have a rekeying fee in the terms.
