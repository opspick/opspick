Reduce phone calls with a FAQ page that actually answers

Most repetitive calls are not questions about your business. They are questions about parking, price ranges, and whether you can fit them in this week — and all three can be answered without you picking up.

Find out what people actually call about

Do not write from imagination. For two weeks, keep a tally sheet next to the phone and write four words for every call: who, what, when, resolved. At the end you will have a list, and the top five items usually account for most of the volume.

Common repeat calls: availability this week, price, parking or access, which cards you take, and how to change a booking. If your list differs, trust your list.

Notice that only one of these is really a FAQ problem. The rest are a page-structure problem, and fixing the booking widget removes more calls than any amount of writing.

Write answers, not policies

The most common failure is an FAQ that restates the rule without answering the question. "Cancellations are subject to our 24-hour policy" tells the caller nothing they can act on. "Cancel free up to 24 hours before. Inside 24 hours we charge 50%, waived if we refill the slot" answers it.

Three habits make answers useful:

  • Lead with the answer. First sentence, not third paragraph.
  • Give a number or a range. "From $45, depends on length" beats "prices vary".
  • Say what to do next. A link, a button, or a time to call — every answer should end somewhere.

If a price genuinely depends on the case, say what it depends on. "Colour starts at $120; long or bleached hair costs more — send a photo and we will quote" is honest and saves the call.

Put it where the call would have happened

A FAQ page nobody visits reduces nothing. The useful placement is at the point of hesitation: under the booking button, on the confirmation email, in the reminder message, and on the Google Business profile.

Also put the top three answers directly on the booking page as short lines rather than links. People do not click through to a FAQ when they are one tap from booking; they either book or they leave.

Keep the page to one screen on a phone. Group by topic only if you have more than ten; below that, order by frequency.

Answer the price question without lying

Price is the question people are most reluctant to ask and most likely to abandon over. Publishing nothing does not protect your margin; it sends the caller to a competitor who published a range.

What works is a starting price plus the variable: "from $60, typically $60–90 depending on length and density." That is specific enough to qualify the caller, vague enough to be true. It also filters out the people who were never going to pay your rate.

Be careful with the word "from" when the real starting price is higher than the one advertised. The review that follows costs more than the booking was worth. Pricing services with add-ons covers how to present ranges without inviting disputes.

Close the loop on the phone itself

The FAQ handles what can be answered in text. For the rest, make the phone easier: a greeting that states the hours, a voicemail that asks for name, number and service, and a promise about when you will call back — kept.

If you use a booking tool, check whether it can send a self-service reschedule link in the reminder. That single setting removes a large share of "I need to move my appointment" calls, and it is usually free. Booksy and Fresha include it on paid plans; check current pricing. Automated reminders and SMS versus WhatsApp cover the messaging side.

Review it when the calls change

Look at the tally sheet after a month. If a question has gone from the calls, keep the answer. If a new one appears twice a week, add it. If an answer has become wrong — a price rise, a new address, changed hours — fix it the same day, because a confidently wrong FAQ creates a worse conversation than no FAQ at all.

The goal is not zero calls. It is fewer calls about things a page could have said, so the calls you do take are the ones worth your time.
