Deposits vs card on file: which protects your book better

Both exist for the same reason — a booked hour has value and an empty one costs you money — but they ask the client for different things, and clients react to them very differently.

What each one actually is

A deposit is money taken now and applied to the final bill. The client pays part of the price at booking, and that payment is the commitment. A card on file is authorisation without a charge: you store the card, and you may charge a stated fee if the client cancels late or does not attend, under terms they agreed to.

The difference sounds small and is not. A deposit costs the client money today. A card on file costs them money only if something goes wrong. That single fact drives most of the behavioural difference between them.

Where each one wins

Deposits

Deposits are better when the cost of a no-show is real and immediate: long appointments, services that require ordered stock, or a practitioner you pay by the hour. They are also better for first-time clients, where you have no history to judge, and for high-value bookings where a fee you cannot collect would be a serious loss.

They also settle the argument before it starts: a client who has already paid is far less likely to treat the appointment as optional. Deposits also reduce reminders.html">no-shows through selection — people who will not commit tend not to book.

The cost is conversion. Every payment step loses some bookings, mostly on mobile. If your average ticket is small, a deposit can cost more in abandoned bookings than it saves in protected slots.

Card on file

Card on file keeps the booking flow fast, which matters most for repeat clients and short services. It also scales: you set the terms once, and the protection applies to every booking without a payment conversation each time.

The weaknesses are practical. The charge has to succeed — expired cards, low balances and bank blocks all fail at the worst moment. Clients dispute charges they forgot they agreed to, and a chargeback costs you the fee plus a fee. Storing card data also brings obligations, so use a provider that handles it.

The terms matter more than the mechanism. A card on file is only enforceable if the client clearly agreed to a specific fee under specific conditions. Vague wording such as "we may charge for missed appointments" gets reversed.

A mixed policy usually fits best

Most small businesses end up somewhere in between, and that is a reasonable place to land:

  • New clients, long services: deposit at booking.
  • Repeat clients with a clean record: card on file, no charge up front.
  • Clients with a previous late cancellation or no-show: deposit, regardless of history length.
  • Short, low-value services: no deposit, card on file if the tool supports it.

It is easy to explain, and clients recognise the fairness: those who keep their appointments are not asked to prove themselves again.

What to check in the software

Before you choose, confirm three things in whichever tool you use. First, whether the deposit is applied to the final bill automatically or needs a manual note — the manual version causes double charges. Second, whether the cancellation fee can be applied without you typing a card number at the desk. Third, what the refund path looks like and how long it takes.

Square Appointments and Booksy both support stored cards and deposits, with different rules about when each is available on a plan. Fresha handles deposits through its payment flow. Check current pricing and the fee settings on the plan you would actually buy; this is often locked to a higher tier.

Write the wording once

Whichever route you take, the policy text is what you will rely on in a dispute. Say what the amount is, when it applies, whether it is refunded if the slot is refilled, and how the client can see the terms again. Deposit policy templates has copy you can adapt, and no-show fee wording covers the phrasing that holds up when a client pushes back.

There is no universally correct answer. Deposits protect revenue more reliably; cards on file protect the booking flow. Pick based on what actually costs you money when a slot goes empty, then apply it consistently for a quarter before changing it again.
