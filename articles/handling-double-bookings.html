Handling double bookings without losing the client

Two clients, one chair, and both of them holding a confirmation. The way you handle the next ten minutes decides whether this costs you one appointment or two clients.

How it happens

Almost no double booking is caused by carelessness. There are three common routes, and they look identical from the outside.

The first is two calendars. One person books in the software, another writes in a paper diary or a shared phone calendar, and the two are synced late or not at all. The second is a reply that was never entered: a client agrees to a time in a message thread, the staff member treats that as booked, and the slot is not actually taken in the system. The third is a broadcast — a waitlist or a group message offering a freed slot — where two people reply and both are told yes.

Each of these is a process gap rather than a personal failure, which matters because blaming the person on the desk usually hides the gap instead of closing it.

The first ten minutes

When you discover the clash, the order of operations matters more than the wording.

  1. Check the record before you call anyone. Find out which booking was entered first and which one came through a channel that never reached the calendar. That decides who has the stronger claim to the slot.
  2. Call, do not text. A double booking is a service failure, and a phone call is the only channel where you can hear whether the client is flexible or furious.
  3. Offer a specific alternative, with a name attached. "Thursday at 14:00 with me, or Saturday morning with Elif" is a solution. "We'll find you something" is not.
  4. Fix the calendar before you hang up. Both entries should be correct in the system while the client is still on the line. If you fix it later, it will be forgotten.
  5. Decide who absorbs the cost. If the client is inconvenienced through your error, the honest answer is that you do — a waived fee, a complimentary add-on, or priority on their next preferred slot.

Whoever is moved should be the later booking, not the more expensive one. Sorting by revenue is the mistake that turns a quiet apology into a review you cannot answer.

What to say

SituationOpening line
Your error, client flexible"I've made a mistake with the diary and I'd rather tell you now than have you arrive to a problem. Can I offer you…"
Your error, client not flexible"You're booked and I'm honouring it. I'll move the other appointment and cover the difference myself."
Client booked through a channel that was never confirmed"I can see your message, and I can see why you thought it was confirmed. That's on us for not replying clearly."

Do not explain the software. Clients do not care which system failed; they care whether they still have an appointment, and whether you sound in control.

Rules that prevent the next one

Prevention is a short list of rules that everyone follows, even when the shop is busy:

  • One calendar is authoritative. Paper, phone notes and message threads are inputs, never the source of truth.
  • A booking exists only when it is in the system. Verbal and chat agreements get entered before the client leaves or before the conversation ends.
  • One person confirms a broadcast slot. The confirmation comes from the calendar, not from a staff member replying in a thread.
  • Buffer time is part of the appointment, not a gap to be filled. Overlapping buffers is the quiet cause of many "how did this happen" days.
  • End-of-day check: read tomorrow's schedule out loud, once, with the whole team present.

Platforms help with some of this. Booksy, Fresha and Square Appointments all prevent overlapping bookings for a single staff member in their own calendar; check current pricing and, more importantly, check whether they block the slot across multiple staff and locations, which is where most real clashes live. None of them will fix a second calendar kept outside the system.

The review risk

A double booking that is handled well often produces a better review than an ordinary visit, because the client saw you solve something. One that is handled badly produces a review that mentions the word "double booked" and stays near the top of your profile for a long time. The difference is rarely the mistake — it is speed, ownership, and a concrete alternative.

If a deposit policy exists, apply it consistently here. Waiving a fee for a client you inconvenienced is good service; waiving it because the conversation was awkward is how policies stop meaning anything. Written deposit terms make that distinction easier in the moment.
