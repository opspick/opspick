Best CRM for tradespeople: quotes, jobs and follow-ups without spreadsheets

Most trades do not lose money on tools. They lose it on the quote that got no second call, the job that lived in a text message, and the invoice that went out three weeks late.

What a trades CRM actually has to do

The word "CRM" is misleading here. You are not running a sales team with a pipeline of 400 leads. You are running 40 to 150 live relationships a year, in four states: enquiry, quoted, booked, paid. Everything else is decoration.

That changes the shortlist. B2B sales software stores customers and makes you build the workflow; field-service software assumes it, which is why it wins for almost every trade under five vans:

  • An enquiry that turns into a quote with the same reference number, not a new document in a different app.
  • An automatic follow-up at day 3 and day 10 on anything unaccepted. This single feature is worth more than the subscription.
  • A job record that holds the address, access notes, photos and the parts used.
  • An invoice generated from the accepted quote, with payment status visible without opening the bank app.
  • A customer history you can read in ten seconds when they call back eighteen months later.

The short list

Jobber — the best all-rounder for one to five people

Jobber covers quoting, scheduling, invoicing, card payments and automated follow-ups in one place, and its mobile app is the one your apprentice can actually use on site. Plans start around $39/month for a solo operator. Quoting from a phone with photos takes about two minutes, which is the difference between quoting on the driveway and next Tuesday.

Where it grinds: job costing. Material costs are tracked badly, so margin per job still needs a spreadsheet on the lower tiers.

Housecall Pro — the strongest for marketing and repeat service plans

Housecall Pro is built around getting the phone to ring and converting the call: booking widget, review requests, postcard campaigns, and a service-plan product that bills maintenance visits monthly. If your business is heating, plumbing or electrical with a maintenance contract, that plan product alone justifies the price. Entry pricing sits near $79/month, and the useful tiers are higher.

ServiceTitan — for the company with a dispatcher

ServiceTitan is the heavy option: dispatch board, call booking, payroll integration, inventory, price book. It genuinely runs fifteen trucks and is genuinely unnecessary at two. Pricing is quote-based, usually hundreds per month.

Pipedrive — if you sell projects, not callouts

For builders, roofers and kitchen fitters, the sale is a six-week conversation with a site visit, a drawing and a revised quote. Pipedrive handles that better than any field-service tool: stages, activity reminders, email sync, and a deal value you can forecast. From about $14/user/month it is cheap, and you keep the invoicing you already have. The gap is scheduling and job costing.

HubSpot free CRM — the honest zero-cost start

HubSpot's free CRM stores contacts, deals and notes, sends email, and logs calls. If you send ten quotes a month and the real problem is forgetting to follow up, this fixes it for nothing. It will not schedule a job, invoice or hold site photos, so treat it as a bridge.

Where each one breaks in month two

  • Jobber: material costs. You will still be adding up receipts at month end unless you upgrade or export to accounting software.
  • Housecall Pro: custom quotes. A non-standard job with fifteen line items is clunkier than it should be, and add-ons add up.
  • ServiceTitan: the setup. Two weeks of configuration before it earns anything, and it punishes a business that does not follow its process.
  • Pipedrive: everything after the yes. No dispatch, no timesheets, no invoices.
  • HubSpot free: everything after the yes, plus a hard nudge toward paid tiers once you need automation.

The three habits the software cannot fix

Quote within 24 hours. Conversion drops sharply after day one, and the customer is collecting three quotes at once. If you cannot quote same-day, send a holding message with a time you will.

Follow up twice, then stop. Day 3 and day 10, by the channel they used. Most CRMs will automate this; the template matters less than actually sending it. If you are writing those messages by hand, start from the quote automation flow instead of reinventing it.

Invoice on the day you finish. Not Friday, not month end. A job that is invoiced a week late is paid a fortnight late, and the chase sequence starts from the invoice date, not the job date.

What to set up first

  1. Four job types only: callout, small job, project, maintenance. Do not build twenty.
  2. One quote template with your terms, deposit rule and an expiry date on the price.
  3. Two automated follow-ups on unaccepted quotes. Nothing else, at first.
  4. A short intake form on the booking link so access notes and parking arrive before you do.
  5. Card or bank payment on the invoice, so the customer pays while you are still on site.

The split is simple: booking software runs the diary, a CRM runs the money. Most trades under five people should buy the field-service tool that does both, and add a separate CRM only when the sale becomes a project. Whatever you pick, a migration checklist saves the customer history you spent years building.

Frequently asked

Do I need a CRM if I only do ten jobs a month?

You need follow-ups, not a CRM. Start with a free contact tool or a spreadsheet with a "next action" column and a date, and move to paid software when you are sending more than fifteen quotes a month.

Can I use my booking software as a CRM?

Partly. Most trades booking tools store customer history and send quotes, but they handle project pipelines and long sales cycles poorly. Use one tool until the sale takes more than two conversations.

What is the cheapest workable stack?

HubSpot free CRM for contacts and follow-up reminders, plus whatever invoicing you already use. That costs nothing and fixes the most expensive problem, which is forgetting to chase a quote.
