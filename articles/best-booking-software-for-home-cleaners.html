Best booking software for home cleaners

Most cleaning businesses do not fail at scheduling one job. They fail at scheduling the same job every second Tuesday for a year without dropping one.

Recurring is the whole business

A one-off deep clean is easy. The money is in the weekly, fortnightly and monthly clients, and that is where most booking tools get thin. You need a schedule that repeats on its own, a client who can see and skip their next visit, and a way to record how to get into the house without writing it in a text message you will lose.

  1. True recurring appointments — every two weeks, with exceptions, not a manual copy-paste.
  2. Client-side skip and reschedule — so a holiday does not become a phone call during a clean.
  3. Per-client access notes — lockbox, key safe, alarm, dog, parking.
  4. Travel buffers — a realistic gap between jobs, not zero.
  5. Quotes by size — bedrooms, bathrooms, frequency, extras, before the first visit.

Travel time is not optional

The most common scheduling mistake in home cleaning is booking back-to-back across town. The job takes two hours, the drive takes thirty minutes, and the next client is already texting. Set a travel buffer on every service — enough for the realistic drive plus five minutes to carry the kit in.

Then cluster by area. If your software lets you see the week as a map or a list by postcode, use it once a week to move one job and turn three awkward drives into one loop. It is dull work that pays better than any marketing.

The three tools, honestly

Square Appointments

The strongest of the three on recurring bookings and on payments, which matters when you want a card on file rather than chasing invoices. If you already take cards on Square, this is the obvious choice. It is built for appointments rather than routes, so travel time is a buffer you set per service, not a map.

Booksy

Recurring appointments, client notes and reminders work well, and the marketplace can bring in one-off cleans. The per-staff monthly fee is the thing to weigh if you run a small team, and the marketplace commission applies to new clients who find you there.

Fresha

Free core, recurring bookings, client records, and payments through their processor. Fine for a solo cleaner building a client list. Less suited to a team that needs to assign staff to specific recurring visits — check that part carefully before you commit a whole roster to it.

Keys, codes and the notes problem

Every cleaning business ends up holding something sensitive: a key, a lockbox code, an alarm PIN, the name of the dog that bites. Put it in the client record, not in a shared note or a WhatsApp thread, and keep it to the minimum you actually need.

A few habits that save trouble. Use a lockbox or key safe where you can, so nobody carries a master key. Write the entry instructions in the same field every time, with the client's own words for the alarm. And when a client cancels, delete the code rather than leaving it in an old record for years. If your tool cannot restrict who sees client notes, that is a reason to look at a different tool.

Quote before you clean

Send a short quote form before the first visit: number of bedrooms and bathrooms, approximate size, pets, frequency, and any extras like inside the oven or fridge. Price by the bracket, not by guesswork on the doorstep. A written quote that matches the first invoice is worth more than a discount.

What I would do

Solo cleaner with regular clients: Fresha for the free recurring calendar, or Square Appointments if you want cards on file from day one.

Small team with a roster: Square Appointments for recurring visits and payments, or Booksy if you also want the marketplace to fill gaps.

If route planning becomes the real bottleneck, that is the point to look at a field-service tool built for it — but most cleaners under five staff can solve it with buffers and a weekly map check.
