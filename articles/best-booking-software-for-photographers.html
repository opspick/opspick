Best booking software for photographers

A photographer's calendar is not a list of thirty-minute slots. It is a set of whole days, a handful of golden hours, and a deposit that has to clear before the date is really gone.

A wedding is not a haircut

Most booking software was designed for services measured in minutes. A wedding occupies a Saturday from getting-ready to the last dance, plus a travel day, plus an editing week that has nothing to do with the calendar. Forcing that into a fifteen-minute grid is possible. It is also how double-bookings happen.

What a photographer actually needs from a booking layer is narrower than a salon's needs and deeper in one place: the money.

  • Whole-day and half-day services — not slots that happen to be long.
  • A deposit that holds the date — and a clear balance due date after it.
  • A contract before payment — signed, stored, attached to the booking.
  • Travel and location — captured at enquiry, not discovered the week before.
  • An enquiry step — because not every shoot should be instantly bookable.

The three tools, honestly

Fresha

Free, polished, and clearly built for walk-in services. You can create a "wedding" service with a long duration and take a deposit, and it will do the job for a portrait photographer with a simple offer. It has no contract, no questionnaire worth the name, and no concept of a date being held pending a signature.

Booksy

Same family of product with better reminders and a marketplace that is almost entirely the wrong audience for wedding work. Per-staff pricing is irrelevant if you are solo, which makes the free tiers more attractive than they first appear.

Square Appointments

The best of the three on money: invoices, deposits, card on file, clean reporting. If you already run your business through Square, the calendar is a reasonable addition. It is still not a client-management tool, and it will not chase a signed contract for you.

Deposits, contracts and the enquiry

The order matters. A couple enquires, you reply, they choose you, they sign, they pay a retainer, and only then is the date theirs. Software that lets someone pay and book in one step skips the conversation, and for a four-figure wedding that conversation is the product.

So the honest setup is usually two layers: a form that captures the enquiry — date, venue, guest count, budget range, how they found you — and a booking tool that handles the deposit once you have said yes. Trying to compress both into one checkout produces bookings you did not agree to and dates you have to give back.

If the tool cannot hold a date as "provisional", use a manual block in the calendar for pending enquiries. It is not elegant. It is, however, the difference between a busy summer and two weddings on the same Saturday.

Mini sessions and the batch day

Mini sessions are the one case where ordinary slot booking works beautifully: one location, one afternoon, eight twenty-minute slots, one price, paid in advance. Set the slots, publish the link, let people self-serve. This is exactly what Fresha and Booksy are good at, and there is no reason to overthink it.

The trap is the weather. Build a backup date into the offer before you publish, and say plainly what happens if it rains. A rescheduled mini day with twelve families is a week of messages if the policy was vague at the start.

What I would do

Portraits and mini sessions, simple pricing, no contracts: Fresha with a deposit on every service. It is free to start and the client-side flow is the least friction you will find.

Weddings and commercial work where the deposit is large: Square Appointments for invoices and card-on-file, with contracts handled by a separate e-signature tool. If you want reminders chasing the balance, Booksy does that well, though the marketplace will send you the wrong kind of enquiry.

If your year is mostly weddings, a photographer-specific studio management tool will beat all three on contracts, questionnaires and delivery timelines. Use a booking tool for the calendar if you like it, but do not expect it to run the business. Check current pricing before you commit to an annual plan.
