Best booking software for physiotherapists

A physio does not sell one appointment. They sell six weeks of them, usually to someone in pain who would rather be somewhere else.

A course of treatment, not a single visit

Booking software built for salons assumes the customer will decide when to come back. Physiotherapy works the other way: the first session ends with a plan, and the plan already has dates in it. The tool has to make booking the next four visits a thirty-second job, not four separate conversations.

  • Initial assessment and follow-up as separate services — different lengths, different prices, different notes.
  • Recurring bookings — the same weekday at the same time for six weeks, with a clean way to stop.
  • Session packages — paid up front, with a visible remaining balance.
  • Room or equipment — the treatment couch, the gym space, the ultrasound machine.
  • Notes attached to the visit — private, and findable next time.

The three tools, honestly

Fresha

Free at the core, easy for patients, and perfectly adequate if you are a solo physio with a room and a card reader. Packages exist. Clinical notes do not, so most people keep them elsewhere. That is a workable split while you are small and an annoying one once you have two clinicians.

Booksy

Strong reminders and a consumer marketplace that occasionally sends you a new patient. Recurring appointments can be set up, but the flow is built around one booking at a time, so a six-week plan takes repeated taps. Per-staff pricing matters again here: associates cost money.

Rooms, equipment and the shared calendar

Two physios and one treatment room is a scheduling problem that general tools handle badly. If the room is a bookable resource, the second clinician cannot take a slot that is already occupied — and you find out at booking time rather than at the door.

If your software cannot do resource booking, the honest workaround is a shared calendar with a hard rule: nothing goes in without the room. That works for two people and falls apart at four.

Equipment adds a second layer. A gym-based session needs the gym, not just the couch. Write down the spaces you actually have before you compare features, because "rooms" is often a paid add-on rather than a checkbox.

Packages, insurance and the 24-hour rule

Most physio revenue arrives in one of three shapes: pay per session, a block of six paid up front, or an insurer paying on your behalf. The first two are easy. The third is where booking tools stop and practice management begins, and pretending otherwise costs you an afternoon a week in reconciliations.

If you take insurance, decide now whether the booking tool is allowed to know about it. If not, keep the two systems separate and make the front desk the bridge. It is less elegant and considerably more reliable than a half-finished integration.

The cancellation rule deserves a sentence on the booking page. Twenty-four hours is the common standard; whatever you choose, apply it without exception for a month, because the first exception teaches every patient that the rule is optional.

One more detail: a patient in pain cancels late because they cannot get there, not because they forgot. A policy that punishes that too hard loses the patient. A policy that ignores it entirely loses the slot. The workable middle is a fee credited against the next visit.

What I would do

Solo physio, one room, mostly self-paying patients: Fresha, packages switched on, notes kept in a separate clinical tool. Cheap, and the patient experience is good.

Small clinic with associates and a shared diary: Square Appointments for the calendar and payments, with clinical records elsewhere, or Booksy if reminders and new-patient discovery matter more than resource booking.

Once you are handling insurer billing or outcome measures, stop looking at booking tools and look at physio-specific practice software. Check current pricing for the seats you need before you decide — the per-clinician fee is usually the number that decides it.
