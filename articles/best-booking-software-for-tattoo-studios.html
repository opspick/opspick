Best booking software for tattoo studios

Tattoo booking is mostly a deposit problem wearing a calendar costume. The software matters less than whether the money arrives before the drawing starts.

A tattoo is not a haircut. The client books a consultation before they book a session, the session can run anywhere from one hour to a full day, and the artist loses real work if someone disappears. That means three things have to work: per-artist calendars, a deposit taken at the moment of booking, and a consultation that exists as its own short appointment rather than a long text thread.

What to look for

  1. One calendar per artist — not one studio calendar with names typed into the notes.
  2. Deposits at booking — card payment up front, clearly non-refundable, applied to the final price.
  3. Consultation as a service — 15 to 30 minutes, bookable, with its own intake questions.
  4. Long sessions that block properly — four- and six-hour slots that do not let a second client slip underneath.
  5. Consent and aftercare attached — signed before needles, sent again after the session.

The three tools, honestly

Booksy

Handles per-staff calendars well and supports deposits and forms on the booking flow. Marketplace discovery is a genuine bonus for a studio in a city with foot traffic. The monthly per-artist fee adds up quickly in a shared studio, where artists often pay their own way — check whether the plan lets each chair carry its own subscription.

Fresha

Free core with per-staff calendars and a marketplace. Good for a new studio with one or two artists. Deposits run through their payment processing, and the refund rules are the part to read carefully, because your deposit policy has to match what the software will actually let you do.

Deposits: say the rule once, clearly

The deposit is not a penalty, it is a booking fee that becomes part of the tattoo price. Write it that way on the booking page: amount, whether it is refundable, how much notice you need to reschedule, and what happens if the client arrives late or changes the design. One short paragraph beats a wall of terms nobody reads.

Two practical rules keep it fair. Apply the deposit to the final price so the client never feels double-charged. And let people move the appointment once with reasonable notice — a strict "no refunds, no moves" policy reads as distrust before the first conversation.

Make the consultation a real appointment

Most of the back-and-forth in a tattoo studio happens because the consultation lives in DMs. Give it a slot: 20 minutes, in person or a video call, with a short form for placement, approximate size, reference images and budget. It filters out the clients who were never going to book, and it gives the artist something to quote from.

Attach the consent form to the session confirmation, not the consultation. People sign it closer to the date, and the answers are more accurate once the design is settled.

What I would do

Solo artist or a two-chair studio: Fresha with deposits on every new booking and a 20-minute consultation service.

Larger studio where artists share a space: Booksy for per-artist calendars and the marketplace, or Square Appointments if the studio already runs everything through Square.

Then stop taking bookings by DM. The calendar is only as good as the rule that everything goes through it — including the friend of a friend who "just wants a small one".
