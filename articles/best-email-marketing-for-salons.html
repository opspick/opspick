Email marketing for salons: building a list without spamming it

You do not need a campaign calendar. You need four emails tied to things the client already did.

Why most salon email lists die

The pattern is familiar. A shop collects a few hundred addresses, sends a monthly newsletter with a discount, and within four months the open rate is flat and the unsubscribes are climbing. Nothing was wrong with the writing. The list was built on the wrong promise.

Clients give a salon their email for one reason: to make the next appointment easier. Every message that does not serve that reason is a withdrawal from the account. Discount blasts spend the balance fast.

Building the list with consent you can prove

Collect the address at booking, on the booking flow, with a box the client ticks. Not a pre-ticked box, not a scraped address from an old spreadsheet, not a name you took off a receipt. Consent that cannot be shown on request is consent you do not have, and inbox providers treat it accordingly.

  • Where to ask: the booking form, directly under the phone number field.
  • What to promise: "Appointment reminders and the occasional note when something changes at the shop." That is a promise you can keep.
  • What not to promise: "exclusive offers" unless you intend to send them forever.
  • What to do with old lists: send one re-permission email. Anyone who does not click stays off.

Both Booksy and Fresha collect client contact details in the booking flow and expose marketing consent separately from reminder consent; keep those two switches apart, because they are legally and practically different. Square links its marketing list to the client directory for the same reason. Check what each tool does with a client who books as a guest - that is where consent usually gets lost.

The four emails that carry their weight

Confirmation and prepBooking madeSet expectations: arrival time, parking, what to bring

Thank you and review askDay after the visitRequest a review while the visit is fresh
Win-back8-12 weeks since last visitRecover a client who drifted

Four messages, all triggered by client behaviour, no calendar needed. This is not marketing in the promotional sense; it is the operational layer of the relationship. It also happens to be the only email most clients will ever open from you.

The win-back is the one that pays for the list. Set it on last-visit date, not on a fixed date, so a client who comes every five weeks never receives it and a client who vanished in March does. Most booking tools can build this segment from last appointment date; if yours cannot, export the client list and filter it manually once a month.

Cadence matters as much as content: one operational email per visit plus one optional seasonal note is enough. A salon that emails weekly trains clients to ignore it, and the reminder - the only email that protects revenue - gets buried with the rest. If you want to send something between visits, send it to a segment, never to the whole list: clients who booked a colour service, clients due for a cut, clients who have not been in since the season changed.

Deliverability basics that salons skip

Three things decide whether your reminder lands in the inbox: the sending domain is authenticated, the list has no purchased or scraped addresses, and people open the messages. The third one is the reason the first two matter. A list full of addresses that never open gets filtered for everyone on it.

Clean the list twice a year. Remove hard bounces immediately, and suppress anyone who has not opened anything in twelve months - they are not reading, and they are costing you placement. This feels like shrinking your reach. It is actually protecting the reach that works.

If email is doing the retention work, the review request is the natural next step: see automating review requests for the ask that converts.

The short version

Collect consent at booking with a box the client ticks. Send four behaviour-triggered emails and almost nothing else. Segment the occasional extra note instead of broadcasting it. Clean the list twice a year. A small list that opens beats a large one that does not, and the reminders alone justify the setup.

Frequently asked

How often should a salon email its list?

Once per visit for operational messages, plus at most one seasonal note to a segment. Weekly broadcasts wear out the list fast.

Can I add clients to the list without asking?

No. Consent collected at booking, with a tick box, is the only version that holds up - and the only one inbox providers reward.
