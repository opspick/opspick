Training staff on booking software without a classroom

Nobody learns a booking system from a manual. They learn it by doing four tasks on a quiet afternoon, with someone nearby who knows the answer.

Teach the four tasks that cover the week

Most booking platforms have dozens of settings and five things that happen every day. New staff do not need the settings. They need to be able to take a booking, move a booking, take a payment, and explain a policy without guessing.

Structure the first session around those four actions, in that order. Everything else — reports, service categories, staff permissions, loyalty settings — can wait until the person has done a full week with the system in front of them.

A first-week plan

WhenWhatDone when
Day one, 30 minLog in on their own account; find today's schedule; read one client recordThey can find any appointment by name without help
Day one, 30 minTake a booking end to end, including the confirmationThey can book a real client while you watch
Day twoMove, cancel and refill one appointmentThey know where the cancellation fee lives
Day threeTake a deposit or mark a paymentThey can explain the difference between paid and pending
Day fiveRun the day alone, with you reachableNothing on the schedule needs correcting afterwards

Each row is short on purpose. A two-hour induction in a back room produces notes nobody reads; five short sessions on real clients produce a habit.

Use the system's own sandbox

Most platforms let you create a test appointment, a dummy client, or a staff account that does not send real messages. Use it. Let the new person make the mistakes there — double book a slot, cancel the wrong appointment, send a reminder twice — and see what the system says. The error messages are the best training material you have, and they are free.

Booksy, Square Appointments and Fresha handle test data differently, and some will send real notifications from a test booking; check current terms and pricing, and confirm the sandbox behaviour before you hand it to a new starter.

What to skip in week one

  • Report building and export formats — nobody needs these before they can take a booking.
  • Every notification template — teach the two that fire automatically and leave the rest.
  • Pricing configuration — a new starter should not be able to change prices on day two.
  • Integrations and API settings — these are owner-level tasks, not front-desk tasks.

Write the five rules on the wall

Software training fails when the process around it is unclear. Before teaching the buttons, agree on the rules the buttons enforce. A short list, printed and visible, prevents most of the questions that would otherwise interrupt a client:

  1. A booking exists only when it is in the system.
  2. Never promise a slot you have not seen on screen.
  3. Take payment or a deposit before the appointment, per policy.
  4. If a client asks for something the system will not allow, escalate — do not improvise.
  5. Note changes in the appointment, not in a message thread.

These five also make a good interview question. Someone who can explain why rule two exists will learn the software faster than someone who has used a similar platform before.

Check that it stuck

Observation beats testing. A week after training, watch one booking being taken without intervening, and look for three things: did they check availability before speaking, did they confirm the time back to the client, and did the appointment appear correctly on the schedule afterwards. Those three cover most of the errors that reach the client.

Then do it again after a month, and once more after a busy period. Skills learned on a quiet Tuesday often disappear on a full Saturday, and the fix is usually a one-line reminder rather than another session.

Keep a single page of notes with the things specific to your setup — where the deposit setting lives, who can issue a refund, which staff account to use for training. Store it with the schedule, not in a folder nobody opens. New starters ask the same six questions, and a written answer saves the same conversation every time.
