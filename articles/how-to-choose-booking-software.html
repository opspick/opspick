How to choose booking software

Most bad software decisions are not caused by missing features. They are caused by choosing before you know which three features your business actually depends on.

Describe the week before you look at demos

Spend an hour writing down how bookings arrive now. Not how you would like them to arrive — how they actually do. Include the phone calls, the Instagram DMs, the regular who texts the owner directly, the client who books two services and expects one slot, and the no-show you absorb every fortnight.

Then mark the three steps that cost you the most time or money. For a barber that is usually the phone and the no-show. For a clinic it is intake forms and deposit collection. For a studio it may be class capacity and waitlists. Those three become your evaluation criteria. Everything else is a tiebreaker.

This step feels obvious and almost nobody does it. Without it, every demo looks impressive because every vendor demonstrates their strongest feature, not yours.

The requirements that usually decide the purchase

  • Service structure. Can one booking hold multiple services, with the right total duration and the right staff assignment? This is where calendar tools fail.
  • Staff and resource logic. Specific-person booking, rooms, equipment, and what happens when two people need the same chair.
  • Money at booking. Deposits, prepayment, packages and gift cards — and whether a refund is one click or a support ticket.
  • Reminders you control. Timing, wording, channel and sender. Fixed templates become a problem within weeks.
  • Client records. Notes, formulas, allergies, photos, and a history that is searchable at the chair.
  • Reporting. Utilisation, no-show rate, revenue per staff member, and an export you can actually open.
  • Data portability. A full export of clients, notes and appointments, available without a support request.

If two vendors tie on your top three, choose on data portability and support responsiveness. Those are the two things you cannot fix after signing.

Score the shortlist instead of ranking it

Weight your criteria, score each vendor out of five, and multiply. The arithmetic is less important than the argument it forces.

CriterionWeightWhat a 5 looks like
Fits your core workflow30%Your three costliest steps are handled without a workaround
Total monthly cost20%Predictable, including payments and message fees
Client experience20%Books in under a minute on a phone, no account required
Data export and ownership15%Full export, documented, no fee
Support and reliability15%Reachable during your opening hours; clear status page

Keep the shortlist at three. More than three and you stop comparing and start collecting brochures.

Run a trial that resembles a normal Tuesday

Do not test with one service and one staff member. Set up your real menu, your real hours and at least two staff. Then, in the trial account:

  1. Book as a new client on a phone, on mobile data, without logging in.
  2. Book two services in one visit and check the duration and price.
  3. Take a deposit, then refund it, and see where the fee lands.
  4. Cancel and rebook, and confirm the reminders adjust rather than duplicating.
  5. Move an appointment between staff and check the calendar updates everywhere.
  6. Export the client list and open it in a spreadsheet.
  7. Ask support a real question and time the reply.

Seven steps, about two hours. They surface most of what a six-month contract would otherwise teach you.

Price the whole thing, including the exit

Add the subscription, payment processing, extra staff seats, SMS credits, hardware and any setup fee. Then ask what leaving costs: notice period, data export fee, hardware that stops working, and whether the client booking page disappears.

Two questions do most of the work here. "Can I export everything, including notes, at any time?" and "What is the notice period?" A vendor that answers both plainly is usually honest about the rest.
