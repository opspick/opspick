Seasonal pricing strategies: peak weeks, quiet weeks and how to publish them

Your calendar is not flat, so your prices should not be either. Seasonal pricing is the cheapest revenue lever you own - if you publish it before clients feel it.

Why flat prices leave money on the table

Most service businesses charge the same on a December Saturday as on a wet Tuesday in February. That single price has to fill empty hours and ration scarce ones at once, and it does neither well. Seasonal pricing splits the job: you raise the rate when demand exceeds capacity and lower the effective cost - through bundles rather than headline discounts - when capacity exceeds demand. Done openly, clients accept both. Done quietly, both feel like a trick.

Find your real peaks before you set a number

Two years of booking history is enough. Export appointments by week and plot revenue and utilisation, not just bookings. You are looking for weeks consistently above 85% utilisation and weeks under 55%.

The pattern is usually narrower than owners expect. It is rarely "summer" - more often the fortnight before a holiday, the first week of a school term, or the Saturdays of wedding season. Trim the definition until it is specific, because a narrow peak justifies a bigger premium.

Write the peak list down. Square Appointments and Booksy both handle date-based pricing on some plans; if yours does not, a rate card on the booking page is enough. Check the current tier before you plan around it.

How much to raise in peak weeks

Ten to twenty percent is the range most clients absorb without comment. Below ten you rarely cover the extra staffing; above twenty-five you need a visible reason, like a guest artist or extended hours.

Two rules keep it defensible. Apply it to time, not people: "Saturdays and the week before Christmas" is a policy, while "new clients pay more" reads as a penalty. And keep regulars on their old rate if they book early - the grandfathering logic in how to raise prices without losing clients turns a price rise into an incentive to book ahead.

Discounting quiet weeks without trashing your average

The mistake is a flat 20% off everything, which trains clients to wait. Better options, in order of how little they cost you:

  • Off-peak time slots. Discount Tuesday to Thursday and mid-afternoon only. You are selling hours you would otherwise write off, and the discount never touches your Saturday rate.
  • Bundles. Two visits for the price of 1.8, or a service plus a retail product at a package rate. Average ticket holds up better than with a straight cut.
  • Prepaid packages and memberships. Cash upfront, commitment for the quiet months. Membership pricing models covers how to structure these so they do not cannibalise full-price bookings.
  • Gift cards in the slow season. Sell them in November, redeem them in February. How to sell gift cards has the expiry and tracking rules.

Whatever you choose, put an end date on it and honour it. A quiet-week promotion with no expiry becomes your new baseline price.

Publish it like a timetable, not an apology

Clients tolerate seasonal pricing when it looks like a timetable and resent it when it looks like improvisation. Publish a one-page rate calendar: peak dates, standard dates, off-peak windows, and the date each change starts. Put it on the booking page, in the confirmation message, and on the wall if you have one.

Then use the same dates everywhere. If the widget charges the peak rate but the price list still shows the old number, the first client who notices assumes the worst.

Announce peak dates four to six weeks ahead with the early-booking rate, and you fill those weeks before the premium matters. Send quiet-week offers to your existing list first - a reactivation message usually beats a public discount and costs nothing in margin. Client reactivation campaigns has the wording.

The numbers to watch after the first season

Track revenue per available hour, utilisation, and the share of bookings made more than two weeks ahead. Rising revenue per hour with flat utilisation means the premium worked. Dropping peak utilisation means it was too high or announced too late. Rising advance bookings means you accidentally built a demand forecast.

Review quarterly and change one variable at a time; the discipline in measuring marketing ROI applies here too.

What not to do

  • Do not surprise regulars at checkout. They do not come back to argue; they just leave.
  • Do not discount peak hours. Empty slots are the product you are clearing, not prime time.
  • Do not hide the change in a fee. A "seasonal surcharge" feels like a trap; a published calendar feels like policy.

Frequently asked

How much can I raise prices in peak season?

Ten to twenty percent is the usual range clients accept without comment. Above twenty-five percent needs a visible reason such as guest specialists, extended hours, or genuinely higher running costs.

Should I discount in quiet weeks?

Discount time slots and bundles, not your headline rate. Off-peak windows, prepaid packages and gift-card pushes clear capacity without teaching clients to wait for a sale.

How far ahead should I announce seasonal prices?

Four to six weeks before the peak dates, with an early-booking rate. Clients plan around published calendars; they resent prices they only discover at checkout.
