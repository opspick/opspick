Membership pricing models for salons and clinics

A membership is a promise about next month, sold this month. The model you choose decides whether that promise is a stable income or a permanent discount you cannot withdraw.

Three models, three different promises

Most memberships are one of four shapes, and each sells a different thing. The mistake is choosing by what sounds attractive rather than what your week can deliver.

ModelWhat the client buysBest fitMain risk
Prepaid credit packFive or ten sessions, used at their paceIrregular clients, high-ticket treatmentsLarge liability if balances are not tracked
Monthly creditsA set number of visits each monthPredictable repeat work, one or two servicesUnused credits pile up and feel unfair
UnlimitedAs many visits as they bookBusinesses with genuinely quiet hoursPeak-hour overuse at a fixed price
Hybrid or tieredA fee plus included visits and a discount on extrasMixed menus, retail, add-onsToo many tiers to explain at the desk

Unlimited is the one to be honest about. It works when an extra visit costs almost nothing — an empty 2pm on a Tuesday, a class that runs anyway. It fails when every extra visit consumes a therapist-hour you could have sold at full price. If your Saturday is full and your Tuesday is empty, unlimited fills Tuesday and clogs Saturday, and you have sold your best hours at your worst price.

Price from capacity, not from the discount

Most membership prices are built backwards: take the single-visit price, subtract a discount, hope the volume covers it. That method never asks what a member costs you, so it cannot tell you whether the plan is viable.

Work forwards instead. Start with the revenue you need from memberships each month, divide it by the number of members you can genuinely serve without displacing full-price bookings, and you have a floor price. Then test that floor against three numbers:

  • Cost per visit. Product, laundry, your own hour, room time. The membership price must clear this on every redemption, not on average.
  • Displaced revenue. If a member takes a 6pm Friday slot that would have sold at full price, the membership is losing money on that visit even when it looks profitable on paper.
  • Breakage you can defend. If the plan only works when a third of the credits go unused, it is a bet against your clients — and the ones who notice leave.

Then keep the entry price simple. One clear plan at a fair price will outsell three clever tiers at a new business, because nobody wants to do arithmetic in a chair. Add tiers later, once you know which services members use; the add-on pricing guide covers the extras a membership usually excludes.

The clauses that decide whether it works

A membership lives or dies on five sentences. Write them before launch, put them on the signup page, and apply them the same way every time.

  • Rollover. Let unused credits carry for one month, then expire. Unlimited rollover turns into a savings account you fund.
  • Pause. Once a year, up to 30 days, requested before the billing date. Pregnancy, surgery and long holidays are real; a pause every other month is a cancellation in disguise.
  • Cancellation. Thirty days' notice, in writing, and no refund of the current month. Clients accept it when it is stated up front.
  • Price increases. Give at least 60 days' notice and hold existing members on the old price for a stated period. Raising the price on a plan someone joined on trust is how you lose the members who used it most.
  • No-shows. A membership visit is still an appointment. If they do not arrive, the credit is used. Say it once, in the terms, and apply it without debate.

Sharing is the sixth, worth deciding even if you never enforce it. One membership used by two people is a discount, not a subscription. Decide whether a named partner is allowed, and put it in the terms.

Launching without breaking the book

Start with one plan, cap the number of members, and sell it to the clients who already come monthly. They gain from it, they are least likely to abuse it, and their loyalty is worth locking in.

Then protect the calendar. Ring-fence the hours the plan may use: off-peak weekdays, or a defined number of member slots per day. Members booking peak hours is the fastest way to make a plan unprofitable, and the fix is far easier before launch. If you are already fighting empty slots, read how to reduce empty slots first — a membership should fill a gap, not create a new one.

Finally, use software that can actually run it. Recurring billing, credit balances and member-only booking rules are the three features that matter: Fresha and Booksy offer membership and package features in some plans, and Square handles recurring payments and packages alongside its calendar. Fees differ by market and plan, so check current pricing before you build a launch around them.
