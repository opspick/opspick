Best booking software for personal chefs: tasting menus, dietary briefs and travel

A private chef does not sell a dinner. They sell a date, a menu agreed by message, a grocery run, two hours of cooking in someone else's kitchen, and a deposit that has to clear before the shopping starts.

Why a restaurant booking tool fails here

Restaurant systems allocate tables. You allocate an evening, a kitchen you have never seen, and a budget that depends on what the market has. The booking is the smallest part of the job; the paperwork around it is the work. Four things have to live in one place:

  • An enquiry with a real date, because two clients will want the same Saturday in December.
  • A menu and a dietary brief, agreed in writing before you shop.
  • A deposit and a final balance, with a cancellation rule attached.
  • Travel and shopping time, which is unpaid unless it is priced in.

The five features that actually matter

  1. Enquiry-to-quote in one thread. Date, headcount, budget, then a menu proposal with a price and an expiry. Not five separate emails in a phone inbox.
  2. Deposit before shopping. A card payment or a bank transfer reference that clears before you buy anything. This is the single biggest protection a private chef has.
  3. Dietary and allergy fields on the booking, visible on the day, not buried in a chat history from three weeks ago.
  4. A calendar that blocks travel. A Tuesday dinner ninety minutes away is not a Tuesday you can also book at home.
  5. Repeat-client history. What you cooked, what they liked, what they asked you to skip. This is what turns one anniversary into four dinners a year.

The short list

HoneyBook - the best all-rounder for a solo chef

HoneyBook was built for event professionals and fits private dining almost exactly: a branded enquiry form, a proposal the client can accept with one click, a contract, an invoice and a deposit in the same flow. Menus can be attachments or line items, and the project timeline keeps the shopping list next to the booking. It is not a calendar-first tool, so you still need a separate diary for travel blocks, and the monthly price is real money for someone cooking four dinners a month.

Dubsado - the cheaper version of the same idea

Dubsado does forms, proposals, contracts, invoices and a client portal, with more automation and a lower entry price than HoneyBook. The trade-off is setup time: workflows are powerful and unforgiving, and a half-configured pipeline is worse than a notebook. Budget an afternoon, build one workflow - enquiry, quote, deposit - and ignore the rest for a month.

Tock - the right tool if you host ticketed dinners

If you run public supper-club nights rather than in-home dinners, Tock sells seats, takes prepayment and manages capacity. It solves a different problem - many guests, one venue - and is the wrong tool for a single family's kitchen. Private chefs often end up using both: Tock for events, a proposal tool for clients.

Square Appointments or Calendly - a stopgap, nothing more

Square Appointments is free for one person and takes card deposits; Calendly publishes availability in ten minutes. Neither understands a menu, a dietary brief or a quote. Use one for a fortnight, then move before twenty clients.

Pricing the parts nobody sees

Most private chefs undercharge because the quote covers food and cooking, and forgets the shop, the packing, the drive and the second trip for the thing the market did not have. Build the price from four numbers: ingredients at cost plus a margin, a per-head cooking fee, a travel fee that is stated openly, and a shopping and prep fee for the hours before the doorbell. The travel line is the one clients question, so put the rule on the quote rather than in a conversation - the same logic as pricing any mobile service.

Deposits deserve the same treatment. A fixed percentage holds a date; a card on file holds a balance. Write the rule once, in the deposit policy, and attach it to every proposal so it is signed rather than argued.

The brief that prevents a bad dinner

Send a short form with the deposit confirmation: allergies, dislikes, how adventurous the table is, whether children are eating, kitchen equipment available, and what time they want to sit down. Five fields, under two minutes, same day as the booking. The reasoning is the same as any client intake form - ask only what changes what you cook - and it saves the phone call at 4pm on the day.

What to set up first

  1. One enquiry form that captures date, headcount, budget and postcode before any conversation.
  2. A proposal template with the menu, the price, the deposit rule and a seven-day expiry.
  3. Travel blocks in the calendar, entered the moment a date is agreed.
  4. A note on every client record: what you cooked, what worked, what to skip next time.

Start with HoneyBook if you want the proposal and deposit handled this week. Choose Dubsado if you will actually build the automation and want to pay less for it. If you are still quoting from your phone by voice note, the tool is not the problem - the quote flow is, and fixing that costs nothing.

Frequently asked

How much deposit should a private chef take?

Enough to cover ingredients and the date: commonly 30-50% at booking, with the balance due 48 hours before the dinner. The deposit must clear before you shop.

Do I need booking software for four dinners a month?

You need a proposal, a deposit and a client record. Four dinners a month is roughly one wasted evening per mistake, so a $20-40 tool pays for itself the first time a date double-books.

Can I just use Calendly and invoices?

For a first month, yes. It cannot attach a menu, collect a dietary brief or hold a signed deposit rule, so the admin comes back as soon as you have two enquiries in a week.
